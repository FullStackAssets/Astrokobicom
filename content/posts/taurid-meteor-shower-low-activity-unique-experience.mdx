The Taurid meteor shower peaks in early November but offers only a handful of meteors per hour. This year the peaks fall around Nov 4‑5 and Nov 11‑12, with a new moon on Nov 9 providing dark skies. Despite a zenith hourly rate of roughly five under ideal conditions, the shower’s relatively slow entry speed of 18 mi (29 km) s⁻¹ can produce bright fireballs that linger in the night. Because the activity is low, observers are encouraged to treat the Taurids as a chance to practice patient sky‑watching rather than a guaranteed fireworks display.
<Callout type="takeaway">The Taurids reward patience more than numbers, turning a sparse shower into a mindful stargazing experience.</Callout>
## What happened
The Northern and Southern Taurids reach their peaks around November 4‑5 and November 11‑12, each delivering roughly five meteors per hour when conditions are optimal. Their entry speed of about 18 miles (29 kilometers) per second is slower than most showers, which allows occasional larger fragments to blaze as fireballs.
For observers in the Northern Hemisphere the radiant sits in Taurus near the Pleiades. The best viewing window is after evening twilight, when Taurus rises in the east and climbs higher through the night. This year the new moon on Nov 9 leaves only a thin waxing crescent, so the sky remains dark throughout the peak nights.
Although the radiant is fixed, Taurid meteors can appear anywhere in the sky, so scanning a wide area—especially east or southeast—maximizes the chance of spotting a fleeting streak.
## Why it matters
The low meteor count forces stargazers to shift focus from counting hits to appreciating the broader night sky, fostering a more contemplative observing habit. The occasional fireball offers a dramatic payoff that can inspire interest in meteoroid science and the parent comet 2P/Encke. Dark‑sky conditions created by the new moon also highlight the importance of timing and light‑pollution awareness for all astronomical pursuits.

## How to think about it
Plan to spend at least an hour outside during the peak nights, preferably after the moon sets. Point your eyes eastward where Taurus rises, but keep a broad scan of the entire sky to catch meteors that wander away from the radiant. Let your eyes adapt to the darkness, avoid bright screens, and enjoy the surrounding constellations—Taurus, the Pleiades, and Auriga—rather than obsessing over a meteor count.
<Callout type="warning">Do not expect a steady stream of meteors; the Taurids can go silent for long stretches.</Callout>
