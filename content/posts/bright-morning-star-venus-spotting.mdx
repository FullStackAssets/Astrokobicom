If you’ve ever glanced east before sunrise and wondered about the dazzling point of light that outshines the stars, you’ve been looking at Venus. After slipping between Earth and the Sun on Oct 24, the planet has vaulted into the morning sky and is now visible for nearly two hours before dawn. Its brilliance will climax on Nov 29 at magnitude ‑4.9, making it easily the brightest object in the pre‑dawn heavens. For anyone who enjoys a quick celestial spectacle on the way to work or school, this “morning star” is hard to miss.
<Callout type="takeaway">Venus is the brightest pre‑dawn object right now and will peak at magnitude ‑4.9 on Nov 29.</Callout>
## What happened
Venus passed through inferior conjunction on Oct 24, moving from the evening to the morning sky. Since then it rises more than 90 minutes before sunrise and gains about three minutes of rise‑time each day.

By Dec 17 the planet will rise 3 hours 50 minutes before sunrise, staying in darkness for over two hours. Its peak brilliance on Nov 29 reaches magnitude ‑4.9—about 12 times brighter than Jupiter and 25 times brighter than Sirius.

Under dark rural skies Venus can even cast a faint shadow, and it remains visible as a tiny white speck against the blue daylight sky after sunrise.
## Why it matters
The planet’s dazzling display offers a free, naked‑eye spectacle that can spark public interest in astronomy and provide a natural clock for early‑morning commuters. Its visibility also harks back to historical “morning star” sightings that guided navigation and inspired cultural references, reminding us how planetary motions continue to shape human experience.

## How to think about it
Check a planet‑watching app for Venus’s exact rise time each day, then position yourself facing east‑southeast before that time. In rural areas, a dark sky will reveal its steady glow; in cities, look for a steady white point that outshines stars. Keep the planet low on the horizon and avoid staring at the Sun while scanning the sky.
<Callout type="warning">Do not look directly at the Sun while searching for Venus; keep your eyes safe and only observe the planet low on the horizon.</Callout>
