After eight years traveling to the inner Solar System, the Mercury Imaging X‑ray Spectrometer (MIXS) aboard BepiColombo finally opened its eyes to the cosmos. In September, the instrument performed a 39‑hour observation campaign of the Crab Nebula, executing 55 spacecraft slews. The data provide the first space‑based calibration of MIXS and a benchmark for its upcoming Mercury surface mapping. Demonstrating that the spectrometer’s angular resolution is marginally better than its 2015 laboratory measurements, the results boost confidence in the mission’s scientific return.

<Callout type="takeaway">MIXS has successfully imaged an astronomical X‑ray source, confirming its performance after an eight‑year journey.</Callout>

## What happened
The MIXS team used the separation of the Mercury Transfer Module from the science orbiters in early September to point the instrument toward the Crab Nebula, a bright X‑ray source 6,500 light‑years away. Over two days (Sept. 11–12) the spacecraft executed 55 slews, gathering 39 hours of exposure that allowed scientists to map how the nebula’s image spreads across the detector.

By comparing the on‑orbit point‑spread function with the laboratory baseline from 2015, researchers found the angular resolution to be slightly sharper than expected. This improvement suggests that the instrument’s optics have remained stable, or even benefited from the long cruise, providing a solid foundation for high‑resolution X‑ray spectroscopy of Mercury’s surface.

## Why it matters
Accurate calibration of MIXS is essential for interpreting elemental maps that will be generated once the spacecraft enters Mercury’s orbit. A well‑characterized point‑spread function ensures that surface compositional variations are not blurred, improving the fidelity of scientific conclusions about Mercury’s geology and exosphere. Moreover, confirming instrument health after an eight‑year interplanetary trek validates the design choices for future deep‑space payloads that must endure long durations before reaching their targets.

## How to think about it
Scientists should update MIXS’s point‑spread function in their data‑reduction pipelines before the Mercury orbital phase begins. Modelers can incorporate the slightly tighter resolution into simulations of elemental abundance maps, which will reduce systematic uncertainties. Mission planners can use this successful calibration as a case study when designing long‑duration cruise phases for future probes, emphasizing the value of early‑flight performance checks.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume MIXS’s calibrated performance will remain unchanged after Mercury orbital insertion; final verification will be required once the spacecraft settles into its science orbit.</Callout>
