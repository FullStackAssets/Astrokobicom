In 2024 a commercial Earth‑imaging satellite captured a clear view of a Chinese winged spacecraft orbiting Earth. The vehicle, bearing a striking resemblance to the U.S. Air Force’s X‑37B, appears to be a secret spaceplane that China has been flying since 2020. Analysts say the image, released in a Defense Intelligence Agency threat assessment, confirms that Beijing can operate reusable, low‑Earth‑orbit platforms. This development could reshape the balance of power in space, where stealth and endurance are prized. Understanding the capabilities of this craft is essential for policymakers and the space community.

<Callout type="takeaway">China now demonstrably operates a reusable, X‑37B‑style spaceplane in low‑Earth orbit.</Callout>

## What happened
The Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA) published an unclassified assessment that included a high‑resolution photo taken by Vantor, a commercial imaging satellite owned by Maxar, in 2024. The picture shows a winged vehicle with delta wings, payload bay doors, a dark heat shield, and a rear‑mounted service module equipped with solar arrays—features that closely match the U.S. X‑37B design.

China has launched four of these spaceplane missions since 2020, each lasting up to nine months. The fourth mission, launched in February 2024, remains in orbit as of the DIA report. While the exact number of vehicles in China’s inventory is unknown, the repeated launches suggest an operational program rather than a one‑off experiment.

Like the X‑37B, the Chinese craft lifts off inside a conventional rocket’s payload shroud, operates autonomously as a satellite, and is expected to re‑enter and land on a runway after its mission, though no landing has been publicly documented.

## Why it matters
The ability to field a reusable, low‑Earth‑orbit platform gives China a stealthy foothold for a range of missions, from technology demonstration to potential directed‑energy weapons, as hinted by the DIA’s broader threat assessment. Reusability reduces launch costs and extends operational presence, challenging the United States’ long‑standing advantage in space‑based assets.

Because the vehicle’s payload and mission profile remain classified, its presence introduces uncertainty into strategic calculations. Allies and adversaries alike must now consider the possibility of covert surveillance, on‑orbit experimentation, or even kinetic capabilities that are difficult to track.

The revelation also underscores the growing role of commercial Earth‑observation satellites in space situational awareness, blurring the line between civilian imaging and intelligence gathering.

## How to think about it
Treat the Chinese spaceplane as a new class of strategic asset rather than a one‑off curiosity. Track its orbital parameters, launch cadence, and any changes in design that appear in subsequent imagery. Incorporate its potential capabilities into broader space‑policy discussions, especially those concerning weaponization and dual‑use technologies. For hobbyists and researchers, consider contributing to open‑source tracking databases to improve collective situational awareness.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the spaceplane is armed; its payload remains classified and may be purely experimental.</Callout>
