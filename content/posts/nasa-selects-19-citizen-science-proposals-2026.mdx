Volunteers have long been a backbone of NASA’s research, and this year the agency is expanding that partnership. On October 28, 2026, NASA’s Science Mission Directorate announced the selection of 19 new citizen‑science proposals for the 2026 cycle. The projects span everything from hunting free‑floating planets with SPHEREx data to classifying galaxy mergers in simulated images. By funding these ideas, NASA is turning everyday enthusiasts into contributors to cutting‑edge astrophysics. The move promises fresh discoveries and a deeper public connection to space science.

<Callout type="takeaway">NASA’s new citizen‑science portfolio will let volunteers directly shape research on planets, galaxies, and more.</Callout>

## What happened
NASA’s Citizen Science Seed Funding Program (CSSFP‑25) received a flood of submissions, and the agency selected nineteen proposals to receive seed funding, including twelve brand‑new projects. The announcement highlighted recent milestones such as the Backyard Worlds: Planet 9 volunteers discovering most of the known brown dwarfs and the Zooniverse platform reaching one billion classifications, underscoring the growing impact of public participation.

Among the selected projects, the “Rogue Worlds” effort will enlist volunteers to search SPHEREx near‑infrared maps for free‑floating planets and brown dwarfs in young stellar clusters. Another proposal, led by Kameswara Bharadwaj Mantha, asks participants to classify realistic mock images of galaxy pairs and tidal features to refine merger‑timescale calibrations. Additional projects aim to develop new tools that blend artificial intelligence with human insight, allowing volunteers to build data pipelines and co‑author scientific papers.

## Why it matters
Expanding the citizen‑science portfolio multiplies NASA’s data‑analysis capacity, especially as missions generate petabytes of imaging and spectral data. Engaging the public not only accelerates discovery but also democratizes science, fostering a more scientifically literate society. The selected projects target high‑impact questions—from the prevalence of rogue planets to the dynamics of galaxy evolution—areas where large‑scale visual classification can reveal patterns that automated algorithms might miss.

## How to think about it
If you’re interested in joining a NASA citizen‑science project, start by visiting the official project pages on the NASA Science portal or the Zooniverse site. Read the tutorial materials carefully; most projects provide step‑by‑step guides and example classifications. Allocate a regular, manageable time slot—consistency often yields higher-quality contributions than occasional bursts. Track your progress, engage with community forums, and consider learning basic data‑analysis skills to deepen your involvement. Remember that every vetted classification adds value, even if it seems modest.

<Callout type="warning">Do not treat volunteer classifications as final scientific results without expert verification and peer review.</Callout>
