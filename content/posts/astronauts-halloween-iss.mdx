When the calendar flips to October 31, the International Space Station becomes a floating stage for Halloween festivities. Astronauts aboard the orbiting laboratory don homemade costumes crafted from spare fabrics, tools, and other supplies found on the station. The tradition, now several years old, lets crew members express creativity while living in the confined environment of microgravity. Beyond the fun, these celebrations highlight how human culture adapts to life far from Earth.
<Callout type="takeaway">Costume celebrations on the ISS show that morale‑boosting traditions can thrive even in the most technical of habitats.</Callout>
## What happened
Astronauts on the ISS have been celebrating Halloween for several years, despite the lack of traditional trick‑or‑treaters at the station’s hatch. Crew members either dress individually or coordinate group themes, creating costumes that range from spooky to whimsical using materials that are already aboard, such as fabric scraps, zip ties, and printed labels. The outfits are photographed and shared on NASA’s channels, giving the public a glimpse of daily life in orbit.
In addition to the visual fun, NASA maintains an archive of past holiday celebrations, underscoring that the station remains a vital scientific platform while also supporting the human need for cultural rituals. These activities coexist with the ISS’s core research missions, reinforcing the station’s role as a laboratory for both science and human factors.
## Why it matters
Celebrating holidays in space serves more than entertainment; it sustains crew morale during long‑duration missions, a critical factor for psychological health. Publicly sharing these moments humanizes the astronauts, fostering public interest and support for space exploration. Moreover, the ingenuity required to fashion costumes from limited resources illustrates problem‑solving skills that are valuable for future deep‑space habitats.

## How to think about it
Mission planners should schedule cultural events like Halloween as part of the crew’s psychological support program, allocating time and resources while conducting a safety review of any props. Teams can develop a simple checklist: verify that costume components are non‑flammable, free of loose debris, and do not interfere with equipment. By treating such celebrations as structured activities, agencies can reap morale benefits without compromising mission safety.
<Callout type="warning">Never attach costume pieces to external hardware or use flammable materials; unsecured or hazardous items can damage equipment or endanger the crew.</Callout>
