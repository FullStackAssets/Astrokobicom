Japan’s Martian Moons eXploration (MMX) spacecraft is slated for an October 19, 2026 launch on an H3 rocket, aiming to land on Phobos, collect surface material, and bring the first samples from a Mars moon back to Earth by 2031. The mission will spend roughly three years orbiting Phobos, deploying the IDEFIX rover and a suite of spectrometers before a fly‑by campaign at Deimos and a return cruise that targets an Australian landing site. If successful, MMX will become the first vehicle to return from Mars orbit and the first to deliver Martian‑system material to laboratories on our planet, opening a new chapter in planetary science.

<Callout type="takeaway">MMX will deliver the first ever samples from a Martian moon, reshaping our understanding of the Mars system.</Callout>

## What happened
The MMX spacecraft launched from Tanegashima Space Center on an H3 rocket at 3:41 p.m. EDT on October 19, 2026. After a ten‑month cruise to the Red Planet, the probe entered Mars orbit and began a three‑year campaign focused on Phobos, where it will deploy the miniature IDEFIX rover equipped with cameras, a Raman spectrometer (RAX), and a miniaturized radiometer (miniRAD). The main exploration module also carries NASA’s MEGANE gamma‑ray and neutron detector and other instruments to map composition and temperature.

Following sample acquisition, MMX will perform a series of flybys of Deimos before firing its return module toward Earth in November 2030. The sealed sample‑return capsule is scheduled to splash down in Australia in 2031, where it will be retrieved for analysis.

## Why it matters
Returning material from Phobos gives scientists a direct window into the building blocks of the Mars system, helping to test whether the moons are captured asteroids or remnants of a giant impact. The composition data could also refine models of early solar‑system dynamics and inform future crewed missions that may use Martian moons as staging points. International partners from CNES and DLR contribute technology and expertise, demonstrating a collaborative pathway for ambitious deep‑space endeavors.

## How to think about it
For enthusiasts, treat MMX as a case study in staged deep‑space missions: launch, cruise, orbital operations, surface interaction, and Earth return. Track each phase through agency briefings, and consider how the scientific questions—moon origin, solar‑system history, resource utilization—fit into broader exploration strategies. When the samples arrive, expect a multi‑year analysis pipeline that will publish results across planetary science, chemistry, and astrobiology.

<Callout type="warning">The mission’s success hinges on flawless rover deployment and sample containment; any failure could prevent the return of material altogether.</Callout>
