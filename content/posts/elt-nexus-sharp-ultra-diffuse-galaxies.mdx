The Extremely Large Telescope (ELT) is slated for first light in March 2029, and its suite of instruments is already promising breakthroughs. Among them, the NEXUS@SHARP spectrograph is designed to capture faint light from the sparsest galaxies in the universe. Researchers have outlined how this capability will let astronomers trace globular clusters within ultra‑diffuse galaxies (UDGs), a class of objects that appear too puffed‑up for standard dark‑matter models. By measuring the motions of these clusters, the ELT can directly probe the dark‑matter halos that shape UDGs. This approach could finally test whether ΛCDM can accommodate such enigmatic systems.

<Callout type="takeaway">NEXUS@SHARP will let the ELT map globular clusters in UDGs, providing a direct test of dark‑matter predictions.</Callout>

## What happened
A paper in New Astronomy, led by Marco Mirabile of INAF‑OAAb and GSSI, describes a simulation of NEXUS@SHARP observations of ultra‑diffuse galaxies. The authors argue that current spectrographs cannot reach the depth needed to obtain velocities for the sparse globular cluster populations that orbit these galaxies.

Using realistic exposure times and the ELT’s 39‑meter aperture, the study shows that NEXUS@SHARP can obtain high‑resolution spectra for clusters that are otherwise too faint, enabling precise velocity‑dispersion measurements that constrain the mass of the host dark‑matter halo.

The research highlights that once the ELT achieves first light in 2029, NEXUS@SHARP will become a primary tool for probing the dark‑matter content of UDGs across the nearby universe.

## Why it matters
Ultra‑diffuse galaxies sit at the edge of our cosmological models; their apparent mismatch between size and stellar content challenges the ΛCDM framework. By providing direct dynamical mass estimates, the ELT can confirm or refute whether these galaxies reside in massive dark‑matter halos or represent a new class of low‑mass systems. The outcome will influence theories of galaxy formation, the nature of dark matter, and the interpretation of faint‑galaxy surveys.

## How to think about it
When planning ELT observations, prioritize UDGs within a few hundred megaparsecs that already have imaging catalogs identifying candidate globular clusters. Allocate multi‑night blocks to reach the necessary signal‑to‑noise, and combine the spectroscopic data with high‑resolution imaging to refine cluster membership. Use the resulting velocity dispersions to model halo mass under a range of anisotropy assumptions, thereby quantifying systematic uncertainties.

<Callout type="warning">Observations will be limited to UDGs within a few hundred megaparsecs, and halo mass estimates remain sensitive to the assumed orbital anisotropy of globular clusters.</Callout>
