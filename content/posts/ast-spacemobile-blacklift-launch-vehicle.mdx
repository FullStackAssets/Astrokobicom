At a New York event on Nov. 12, AST SpaceMobile unveiled plans for Blacklift, a reusable launch vehicle designed to lift 20 tons to low‑Earth orbit. The company says the three‑stage rocket, built around Firefly Aerospace’s Miranda and Vira engines, could be ready for flights within two years. With a constrained launch market and a growing BlueBird satellite constellation, the move could give the firm its own launch cadence of up to twice a month. If successful, Blacklift would mark a rare vertical integration step for a satellite‑service company.

<Callout type="takeaway">AST SpaceMobile aims to secure its satellite supply chain by building a 20‑ton reusable rocket in just two years.</Callout>

## What happened

During the unveiling, executives highlighted that Blacklift will carry up to five BlueBird satellites per launch, with a payload capacity of 20 tons to LEO. The vehicle will use a three‑stage architecture: seven Firefly Miranda engines on the first stage, two Vira engines on the second, and a single Vira engine on the third. This configuration is intended to keep the first stage low enough for easy recovery and reuse, a departure from the two‑stage designs of most current reusable rockets.

AST disclosed that the rocket will be constructed entirely from carbon‑composite materials, leveraging the company’s experience in satellite manufacturing. Although no physical hardware was displayed, the presentation included engine videos from Firefly and animated renderings of Blacklift. The firm targets a maiden flight within two years and plans to launch twice a month once operational.

## Why it matters

By developing its own launch system, AST SpaceMobile could bypass the bottlenecks and scheduling uncertainties that have plagued satellite operators in a crowded launch market. A dedicated vehicle would allow the company to align launch cadence with its satellite production line, potentially accelerating the rollout of its nationwide broadband service. However, the ambitious timeline and reliance on borrowed engine designs introduce technical and financial risk, especially given the lack of a physical prototype.

## How to think about it

Stakeholders should view Blacklift as a strategic bet rather than a guaranteed solution. Investors might weigh the potential cost savings against the high development risk and monitor milestones such as a static‑fire test or a hardware mock‑up. Industry observers can compare Blacklift’s design choices to existing reusable systems to gauge its technical plausibility. Finally, customers of AST’s broadband service should consider how launch delays could affect service rollout timelines.

<Callout type="warning">The program remains speculative: no hardware has been built, and the two‑year schedule conflicts with typical development timelines for new launch vehicles.</Callout>
