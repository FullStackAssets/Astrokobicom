A new citizen‑science initiative is calling on the public to help locate the Milky Way’s hidden black holes. The Black Hole Hunters project asks volunteers to scan TESS light‑curve data for the subtle brightening that occurs when a black hole lenses its companion star. Astronomers estimate the galaxy has produced over 10 million black holes, yet only about 70 have been identified so far. By crowdsourcing the search, researchers hope to close that gap and accelerate follow‑up observations with larger telescopes.

<Callout type=\"takeaway\">Public volunteers can turn TESS data into a rapid hunt for the galaxy’s missing black holes.</Callout>

## What happened
The Black Hole Hunters team, based at the University of Southampton, has opened a portal where anyone with a computer can examine light‑curve segments from NASA’s Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS). Volunteers look for brief spikes in brightness that match the signature of gravitational microlensing—a black hole passing directly between its companion star and Earth, briefly magnifying the star’s light.

When a candidate event is flagged, the team cross‑checks it against known variable stars and planetary transits, then schedules higher‑resolution observations with ground‑based telescopes to confirm the presence of a compact, invisible companion.

## Why it matters
Identifying the hidden black‑hole population will refine models of stellar evolution, supernova rates, and the sources of future gravitational‑wave mergers. A more complete census also informs how mass is distributed in the Milky Way, affecting calculations of galactic dynamics and dark‑matter estimates. Engaging the public accelerates discovery and builds broader support for astrophysics research.

## How to think about it
Treat each light‑curve segment as a hypothesis test: ask whether the observed brightening fits the expected microlensing shape (smooth rise, symmetric fall) and duration (hours to days). If it does, flag it and note the time stamp. After submission, let the science team handle verification; your role is to provide clean, well‑documented candidates, not to confirm the black hole yourself.

<Callout type=\"warning\">Flagged events are preliminary; do not announce a discovery until the team validates the signal with follow‑up observations.</Callout>

## FAQ
<FAQ>
  <Question q=\"How can I tell if a brightening event is caused by a black hole rather than a planet?\">Microlensing events from black holes produce a symmetric, achromatic brightening that lasts longer (typically several hours to days) than the brief, wavelength‑dependent dips caused by planetary transits.</Question>
  <Question q=\"What tools do I need to participate in Black Hole Hunters?\">All that’s required is a web‑enabled computer and an internet connection; the platform provides an interactive viewer and simple annotation tools.</Question>
  <Question q=\"Why are only about 70 black holes known when millions are expected?\">Most black holes are isolated or in binaries with unfavorable alignments, making them invisible to traditional detection methods; only a tiny fraction produce observable lensing or accretion signatures.</Question>
