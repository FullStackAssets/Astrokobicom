When the iconic T. rex is imagined, most picture a 40‑foot apex predator. A new peer‑reviewed study, however, shows that the hatchlings were only about the size of a domestic kitten. Researchers used synchrotron imaging to measure bone growth patterns in fossils from the University of Saskatchewan. The finding rewrites our picture of how these dinosaurs grew and survived their earliest weeks. Understanding their tiny beginnings helps explain why most T. rex juveniles never made it to adulthood.

<Callout type="takeaway">T. rex hatchlings were kitten-sized, indicating a high-mortality, r-selected life strategy.</Callout>

## What happened
The team led by Nick Longrich (University of Bath) and collaborators at the University of Saskatchewan examined several tiny T. rex fossils using the Canadian Light Source synchrotron. The X‑ray analysis revealed detailed blood‑vessel networks, growth rings, and bone density changes, allowing a precise size estimate of roughly 30 cm in length—comparable to a house cat. The study, published in *Biology* on July 7 2026, also noted that adult T. rex could reach 12 m and weigh up to 7 t, and that females likely laid 30–100 eggs per clutch.

## Why it matters
Knowing that T. rex began life at kitten size supports the idea that they employed an r‑selection strategy: producing many small, vulnerable offspring with little parental care. This explains the high juvenile mortality inferred from the fossil record and informs models of dinosaur population dynamics. It also provides a baseline for comparing growth rates across theropods and modern analogues, sharpening our understanding of dinosaur metabolism and life history.

## How to think about it
When encountering dramatic size claims for extinct animals, consider the method of measurement, the sample size, and the ecological context. Synchrotron data gives high‑resolution insight, but it still represents a snapshot. Compare hatchling size to modern species with known life‑history strategies to gauge parental investment and survival odds.

<Callout type="warning">Size estimates rely on a handful of fossils and assume typical growth patterns; they should not be taken as the exact dimensions of every T. rex hatchling.</Callout>
