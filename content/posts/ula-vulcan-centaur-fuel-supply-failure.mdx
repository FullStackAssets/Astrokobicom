United Launch Alliance's Vulcan Centaur rocket suffered a launch failure on Nov. 15, 2026, when it could not deliver its payload of 40 Amazon Leo internet satellites. The anomaly was traced to the first‑stage booster, where the two BE-4 engines received insufficient fuel, reducing thrust. This marks the first complete loss among the five Vulcan flights to date. The failure revives concerns about the new launch system that is meant to replace Atlas V. Understanding the root cause is critical for both ULA’s schedule and the customers relying on the vehicle.

<Callout type="takeaway">Inadequate fuel flow to the BE‑4 engines caused the Vulcan Centaur’s first‑stage thrust loss and launch failure.</Callout>

## What happened
The Nov. 15 launch from Florida’s Space Coast ended abruptly when the vehicle’s first‑stage thrust dropped, leading to loss of the 40‑satellite Amazon Leo constellation. ULA’s update on Nov. 18 indicated that the low thrust was not due to engine performance but to an inadequate fuel supply to the BE‑4 engines.

The six solid rocket boosters performed nominally, and the Centaur V upper stage with its RL‑10 engine also functioned as expected. The problem appears isolated to the liquid‑propellant feed system feeding the two BE‑4 engines that power the core booster.

Vulcan’s flight history shows recurring issues: the second and fourth flights experienced solid‑rocket‑booster nozzle failures, and this fifth flight is the first to end in total loss. Recent design tweaks to the SRB nozzles had succeeded, but the fuel‑supply anomaly remains under investigation.

## Why it matters
The failure jeopardizes ULA’s timeline for replacing the Atlas V, a cornerstone of U.S. national security and commercial launch capability. Blue Origin, which builds the BE‑4 engines, also faces scrutiny because the same engine type powers its New Glenn vehicle. For Amazon, the loss of 40 Leo satellites delays its broadband‑to‑rural‑areas roadmap and incurs significant financial loss.

A clear diagnosis allows ULA to implement targeted fixes, but the ongoing investigation means launch schedules will stay uncertain, affecting downstream customers and the broader commercial launch market.

## How to think about it
Treat the incident as a reminder that new launch systems require iterative validation of every propellant‑delivery component. Stakeholders should monitor ULA’s forthcoming technical bulletin, assess risk mitigation plans, and consider backup launch options for time‑critical payloads. For engineers, the case underscores the importance of redundancy and real‑time flow monitoring in high‑thrust engines.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the fuel‑supply issue is fully resolved until ULA completes its investigation.</Callout>
