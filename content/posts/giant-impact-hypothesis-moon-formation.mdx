For centuries humanity has gazed at the Moon and wondered how it came to be.  The dominant explanation today is the giant‑impact hypothesis, which posits that a Mars‑sized protoplanet named Theia slammed into the early Earth.  This cataclysmic event, likely occurring within the first 100 million years of the Solar System, would have ejected debris that coalesced into the Moon in just a few hours.  Understanding this origin story matters because the Moon stabilizes Earth’s tilt, drives tides, and influences climate—factors that have shaped life on our planet.

<Callout type="takeaway">A Mars-sized impact is the most supported explanation for the Moon’s birth.</Callout>

## What happened
Theia, a planetary embryo roughly the size of Mars, is thought to have collided with the proto‑Earth at a glancing angle.  The impact vaporized and lofted material from both bodies into orbit, forming a hot, silicate‑rich disk.  Within hours, this disk cooled and the material accreted, giving rise to the Moon we see today.

The idea traces back to 1946 when geologist Reginald Aldworth Daly first suggested an impact origin.  In the 1970s, William Hartmann and Donald Davis refined the concept with numerical models, and by the 1984 Kona conference the giant‑impact scenario had become the leading theory.  Modern isotopic analyses show that Moon rocks share nearly identical stable‑isotope ratios with Earth, bolstering the impact narrative, while the system’s high angular momentum also fits the model.

## Why it matters
If the Moon formed from a giant collision, it explains why Earth’s axial tilt is unusually stable, which in turn moderates climate cycles and supports diverse ecosystems.  The hypothesis also provides a template for how moons may arise around exoplanets, informing the search for habitable worlds.  Moreover, the impact scenario reshapes our view of planetary formation, suggesting that violent collisions are a common step in building planetary systems.

## How to think about it
When evaluating the giant‑impact hypothesis, weigh three evidence pillars: geochemical fingerprints (isotope ratios), dynamical constraints (angular momentum, orbital mechanics), and numerical modeling outcomes.  Recognize that each pillar carries uncertainties, and that new lunar sample analyses or improved simulations can shift the balance.  Use this framework to stay open to refinements while appreciating why the impact model currently leads the field.

<Callout type="warning">Do not treat the giant‑impact hypothesis as an immutable fact; ongoing research may reveal additional complexities or alternative pathways for lunar formation.</Callout>
