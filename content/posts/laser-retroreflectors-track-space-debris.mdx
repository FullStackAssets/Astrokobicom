Space debris now circles the Earth in millions of fragments, threatening operational satellites and crewed missions. In July, British startup Foundational flew its Retro‑2 laser retroreflector on a rideshare payload aboard SpaceX’s Transporter‑17, attaching it to a small satellite carried by D‑Orbit’s ION platform. The 50‑gram device reflected more than 6,111 laser pulses back to ground stations across five low‑Earth‑orbit passes, proving millimeter‑level ranging without any onboard power or electronics. If such passive trackers can be mass‑produced, they could give operators a cheap, reliable way to keep even silent spacecraft on their radar.

<Callout type="takeaway">Passive laser retroreflectors can keep any satellite visible without power or electronics.</Callout>

## What happened
Foundational’s Retro‑2 system consists of seven corner‑cube reflectors mounted on a lightweight housing that weighs about 50 g. During the test, the unit operated at altitudes between roughly 600 km and 870 km, returning laser‑ranging signals to ground stations with millimeter precision. The demonstration proved that a simple, power‑free device can be tracked from Earth over multiple orbital passes.

The test joins a growing toolbox of debris‑mitigation concepts, from large nets and harpoons to active lasering of small fragments. While most proposals focus on removal, Retro‑2 targets the tracking problem: ensuring that every object, even a dead satellite, remains observable for collision‑avoidance calculations.

## Why it matters
Accurate orbital data is the cornerstone of safe space operations. Current tracking relies on radar and optical sensors that struggle with tiny, non‑cooperative objects. A passive retroreflector that can be added to new spacecraft would dramatically improve positional accuracy without draining onboard power budgets. This could extend the usable life of satellites, reduce the need for costly propulsion‑based de‑orbit maneuvers, and give operators more confidence when planning conjunction avoidance.

## How to think about it
Satellite designers should evaluate retroreflectors as a standard payload accessory, especially for low‑cost constellations where power margins are tight. Integrating a retroreflector early in the design avoids retro‑fitting challenges and ensures the device is oriented for optimal laser return. Operators can coordinate with existing laser‑ranging networks to schedule regular passes, building a continuous tracking record that feeds into conjunction‑assessment tools.

<Callout type="warning">Retroreflectors improve visibility but do not eliminate the debris hazard; they must be paired with active removal or end‑of‑life disposal strategies.</Callout>
