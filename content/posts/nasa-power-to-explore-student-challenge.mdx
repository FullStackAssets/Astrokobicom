On November 17, NASA kicked off the Power to Explore Student Challenge, a nationwide STEM competition for kindergarten through 12th‑grade learners. The contest asks participants to illustrate and describe how nuclear “batteries” could power a future lunar base, combining a 200‑word essay with original artwork. Submissions close on March 1, 2027, and winners receive a trip to NASA’s Glenn Research Center. By engaging students early, the program aims to build public understanding of space nuclear power, a technology critical for sustained lunar operations. The initiative reflects NASA’s broader push to establish a permanent presence on the Moon’s shadowed regions.

<Callout type="takeaway">NASA’s student contest uses creative projects to spark the next generation’s interest in nuclear power for lunar exploration.</Callout>

## What happened
NASA announced the Power to Explore Student Challenge on Tuesday, inviting U.S. students from kindergarten to 12th grade to explore space nuclear power systems. Participants must submit a 200‑word essay describing a personal “superpower” that could aid a lunar mission, alongside an original illustration of a nuclear‑powered Moon base that incorporates both radioisotope and fission concepts.

Entries are judged in three grade‑level brackets—K‑4, 5‑8, and 9‑12. One grand‑prize winner per bracket will travel to NASA’s Glenn Research Center for a two‑person visit, while every entrant receives a digital certificate and access to a virtual event with NASA experts.

## Why it matters
Understanding nuclear energy’s role in space is essential as NASA plans to operate in permanently shadowed lunar craters where solar power is unavailable. Early exposure helps demystify the technology, fostering a workforce capable of designing safe, reliable power systems for future habitats and scientific payloads. The contest also supports broader outreach goals, linking classroom learning to real‑world mission challenges and encouraging diverse participation in aerospace engineering.

## How to think about it
Treat the contest as a two‑step learning exercise: first, research how radioisotope thermoelectric generators and small fission reactors have powered past missions; second, translate that knowledge into a narrative and visual that highlights safety, reliability, and mission impact. Use the essay to connect a personal strength—problem solving, teamwork, or curiosity—to a specific power‑system challenge, and let the illustration show how that strength enables a sustainable lunar outpost.

<Callout type="warning">The challenge is open only to students residing in the United States; international students cannot submit entries.</Callout>
