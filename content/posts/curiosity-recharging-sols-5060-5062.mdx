After a week of intensive drilling at the Nazca site, Curiosity entered a brief power‑recovery phase on sols 5060 through 5062. The rover deliberately limited its workload to generate a net positive energy balance, while still gathering remote science data. During this window the team also ran a new engineering test to execute two tasks in parallel, probing ways to stretch the aging power budget. Understanding how the rover conserves energy is critical for extending its scientific return as its solar panels age.

<Callout type="takeaway">Strategic recharging and parallel activity testing let Curiosity keep exploring while preserving dwindling power.</Callout>

## What happened
The three‑sol plan (5060‑5062) was designed to be power‑positive. Each day included a standard morning science block, remote ChemCam observations, Remote Micro‑Imager mosaics of targets like Titicaca, Pampas, and Salta, Mastcam imaging of the calibration and drill targets, Navcam dust‑devil studies, and twilight imaging. The rover also performed drill‑cleanup work, including the RAGE (Rotate to Agitate for Granule Expulsion) maneuver, and prepared additional sample drop‑offs for the SAM and CheMin instruments.

On sol 5061 the team introduced a brand‑new engineering activity to test parallelism during a downlink communications window. By running two low‑impact tasks simultaneously, the rover stays awake for a shorter overall period, which reduces the net power draw—a strategy being evaluated as the rover’s power output slowly declines with age.

## Why it matters
Power is the limiting resource for a solar‑powered rover operating beyond its design lifetime. Demonstrating that Curiosity can regain a battery surplus while still delivering valuable science extends the window for completing the Nazca drilling campaign. The parallel‑execution test provides operational data that could inform future missions, showing how to squeeze more science out of shrinking energy budgets without compromising safety.

## How to think about it
When scheduling rover activities, prioritize power‑positive windows for high‑energy operations and bundle lower‑impact tasks to run in parallel whenever possible. Continuously monitor the battery state of charge and adjust the plan if the projected surplus falls short. Use remote sensing instruments that draw less power to keep scientific output steady while the rover recharges.

<Callout type="warning">Do not add additional high‑power experiments to the same sols without confirming a net power surplus.</Callout>
