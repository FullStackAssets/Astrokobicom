A new type of cosmic radio beacon has been uncovered in the Southern sky. Using the Murchison Widefield Array, astronomers detected bright radio flashes that repeat every 7.9 minutes, but only during a six‑hour window each day. The source is a binary system composed of a white dwarf and a star that closely resembles our Sun. This discovery expands the family of long‑period radio transients and offers a natural laboratory for studying stellar remnants and solar‑type companions.

<Callout type="takeaway">A white dwarf paired with a Sun‑like star produces clockwork radio bursts every 8 minutes.</Callout>

## What happened
Since 2022, researchers have catalogued long‑period radio transients—objects that flash on timescales of minutes to hours. Early examples were linked to red dwarf–white dwarf binaries. In a survey conducted in late 2024 with the Murchison Widefield Array, a team led by Csanád Horváth identified a source that emitted short, bright radio pulses every 7.9 minutes. The bursts appeared for roughly six hours before disappearing for about 26 hours, a pattern that points to an orbital modulation.

Further analysis of archival optical data revealed that the companion star has a temperature and luminosity comparable to the Sun, rather than the cooler red dwarfs seen in earlier systems. The white dwarf, a stellar remnant of a Sun‑like progenitor, likely interacts magnetically with its partner, producing the observed radio flashes as the binary rotates.

## Why it matters
The finding shows that long‑period radio transients are not limited to red dwarf companions; Sun‑like stars can also drive the phenomenon. This broadens our understanding of how magnetic interactions in close binaries can generate coherent radio emission. It also provides a new probe of white dwarf magnetic fields and the plasma environment around solar‑type stars, informing models of stellar evolution and binary dynamics.

## How to think about it
For observers, the key is to combine regular radio monitoring with optical spectroscopy to confirm the nature of the companion. Scheduling observations to cover the predicted six‑hour active phase maximizes detection probability. Theoretical work should explore magnetic reconnection scenarios that can operate on the 7.9‑minute timescale, while accounting for orbital geometry that modulates visibility.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that every long‑period radio transient originates from a Sun‑like companion; many still involve red dwarfs or other configurations.</Callout>
