The ultra‑short‑period exoplanet TOI‑561b, a scorching lava world orbiting its star in less than a day, has surprised astronomers with a surprisingly reflective atmosphere. Using JWST’s NIRSpec instrument, researchers captured phase‑curve measurements that show the planet reflects a large fraction of its star’s light. This finding challenges the expectation that such close‑in rocky planets should be airless and dark, and it opens a new window onto atmospheric formation on extreme worlds.

<Callout type="takeaway">TOI‑561b’s bright reflection indicates a reflective atmosphere where none was expected.</Callout>

## What happened
The team obtained continuous NIRSpec phase‑curve observations of TOI‑561b, measuring the planet’s brightness at multiple points throughout its orbit. The data reveal an albedo far higher than that of typical rocky bodies, implying that a substantial portion of incident starlight is being scattered back into space.

Interpretations focus on a high‑altitude cloud layer composed of silicate vapor or condensates lofted from the planet’s magma ocean. Such a cloud deck would act like a mirror, producing the observed reflectivity while also hinting at a secondary atmosphere generated by volcanic outgassing.

## Why it matters
If ultra‑short‑period planets can retain or regenerate atmospheres despite relentless stellar irradiation, our models of atmospheric loss and planetary evolution need revision. A reflective atmosphere on TOI‑561b suggests that magma‑driven outgassing can replenish gases faster than they are stripped away, offering a pathway for secondary atmospheres on the most hostile worlds. This insight reshapes the search for atmospheric signatures on other close‑in exoplanets and informs assessments of their potential habitability.

## How to think about it
When evaluating ultra‑short‑period worlds, treat albedo as a clue, not a confirmation, of an atmosphere. Combine phase‑curve brightness with emission spectra to disentangle cloud decks from molten surfaces. Compare observed reflectivity against theoretical models of silicate cloud formation and magma‑ocean vapor release. This multi‑pronged approach helps distinguish between a true gaseous envelope and a highly reflective lava surface.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the high reflectivity alone proves a thick atmosphere; surface lava or mineral clouds could produce similar signals.</Callout>
