On November 1 1962 the Soviet Union lifted off the Mars 1 probe, marking humanity’s first attempt to send a spacecraft on a trajectory toward another planet. The probe successfully separated, deployed its solar panels and began its cruise toward Mars, becoming the first human‑made object to reach the vicinity of the Red Planet. However, a malfunction in a gas‑valve that controlled its attitude system caused the spacecraft to lose orientation and, ultimately, radio contact when it was roughly halfway to Mars. The episode highlighted both the promise and the perils of early interplanetary exploration. Today the mission is remembered as a pioneering, if incomplete, step toward the eventual landing of humans on Mars.

<Callout type="takeaway">Mars 1 was the first spacecraft to reach Mars, even though it never completed its mission.</Callout>

## What happened
The Mars 1 probe was launched atop a Molniya‑2 carrier rocket from the Baikonur Cosmodrome. After reaching Earth orbit, the upper stage ignited, placing the spacecraft on a heliocentric trajectory toward the Red Planet. The probe unfolded its solar arrays and began its cruise phase, transmitting telemetry back to Soviet ground stations.

Within days of flight, engineers detected a leak in one of the gas‑valves that powered the attitude‑control system. Without reliable orientation, the antenna could not be pointed toward Earth, and the signal strength deteriorated. Contact was lost when the spacecraft was about halfway along its path to Mars, and no further data were received.

Mars 1 was actually the Soviet Union’s fourth attempt to reach the planet; the three previous launches had failed during ascent. Two years later, NASA’s Mariner IV succeeded in returning the first close‑up images of Mars, building on the hard‑won lessons from the Soviet effort.

## Why it matters
Mars 1 demonstrated that a spacecraft could travel beyond Earth orbit and approach another planet, proving the feasibility of interplanetary navigation. The mission’s failure, however, underscored the critical need for robust attitude control and reliable communications—issues that shaped the engineering of every subsequent Mars probe. In the broader Cold War context, the launch intensified the space race, pushing both superpowers to accelerate their planetary exploration programs.

## How to think about it
Treat early interplanetary missions as experimental milestones rather than finished products. Evaluate them by the technology they introduced, the problems they uncovered, and how those insights fed into later successes. This perspective helps separate the symbolic achievement of reaching another world from the practical goal of gathering useful science.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that reaching a planet guarantees scientific return; early missions often failed before delivering data.</Callout>
