NASA unveiled a new wave of Moon Base contracts on Saturday, signaling a major push toward a permanent outpost at the lunar South Pole. The agency selected eight commercial partners for the next phase of its Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) program and green‑lit a nuclear‑powered rover dubbed PROMISE. Together with a $10 billion, 10‑year CLPS 2 master contract, these moves aim to deliver science payloads, infrastructure and mobility to the Moon before the next crewed Artemis missions. The announcements were made at the MAX POWER aerospace expo at Kennedy Space Center, underscoring NASA’s partnership‑focused strategy.

<Callout type="takeaway">NASA’s new contracts lay the contractual and hardware foundation for a sustained lunar South‑Pole outpost.</Callout>

## What happened
NASA announced eight companies as eligible participants in the CLPS 2 framework, a 10‑year, multiple‑award, indefinite‑delivery/indefinite‑quantity contract with a maximum ceiling of $10 billion. Under CLPS 2, these firms will design, build and operate lunar landers that can transport NASA science, technology and infrastructure payloads to the Moon’s surface, expanding the existing CLPS network.

In parallel, NASA approved development of the PROMISE rover, a nuclear‑powered vehicle intended to travel long distances across the rugged terrain of the South Pole region. The rover’s power system is expected to enable continuous operation through the lunar night, supporting surface exploration and infrastructure construction.

The agency also increased the ceiling of the original CLPS 1 contract from $2.6 billion to $4.2 billion, ensuring funding continuity for early‑stage deliveries while the CLPS 2 contracts ramp up.

## Why it matters
By diversifying the commercial lunar delivery ecosystem, NASA reduces reliance on a single provider and accelerates technology maturation. The infusion of $10 billion over a decade signals long‑term commitment, encouraging private investment and fostering competition that can lower costs. The PROMISE rover adds a new capability: sustained power for surface operations, which is critical for building habitats, extracting resources and conducting science during the two‑week lunar night.

## How to think about it
Stakeholders should view the CLPS 2 awards as a modular supply chain rather than a single‑shot mission. Companies can target niche capabilities—such as precision landing, high‑mass payload delivery, or in‑situ resource utilization—to complement the broader network. For the scientific community, aligning experiment timelines with the anticipated delivery cadence will maximize data return. Finally, policymakers can use the contract structure as a template for future deep‑space procurement, balancing firm‑fixed‑price incentives with performance milestones.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that the first commercial deliveries will occur before 2028; hardware development, testing and certification still require several years.</Callout>
