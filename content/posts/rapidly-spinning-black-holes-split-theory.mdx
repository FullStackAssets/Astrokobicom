Black holes are usually thought of as immutable cosmic sinks, but a fresh theoretical paper asks whether they could ever break apart. Researchers at the University of Southern Denmark have modeled a scenario where an ultra‑fast spinning black hole might divide into two smaller ones. The study, appearing in Physical Review D, shows that entropy and angular momentum constraints make such a split exceedingly rare. If true, the idea reshapes our understanding of black‑hole thermodynamics and the limits of gravitational collapse. While no astronomical evidence exists yet, the work opens a new line of inquiry into extreme gravity.

<Callout type="takeaway">Only black holes rotating near their theoretical maximum could, in principle, split into two.</Callout>

## What happened
The team used analytical calculations of Kerr black‑hole metrics to identify a narrow parameter space where the horizon could become unstable and bifurcate. Their results indicate that when the dimensionless spin parameter approaches the extremal limit (a≈1), the horizon geometry can develop a “pair‑of‑pants” topology that mathematically separates into two distinct horizons.

Entropy provides the primary barrier: a single black hole maximizes entropy for a given mass, so any division would normally increase total entropy, violating the second law of thermodynamics. The authors argue that the extreme rotational energy can offset this penalty, allowing a split that conserves total entropy.

No observational signatures of such events have been recorded, and the authors stress that the conditions required are far beyond those observed in known astrophysical black holes.

## Why it matters
If black holes can split, the principle that black‑hole entropy always grows monotonically would need refinement, impacting theories of information loss and quantum gravity. It also suggests a new pathway for black‑hole mass distribution in dense stellar environments, where near‑extremal spins might be generated through prolonged accretion.

The scenario challenges the conventional view that black holes are end‑points of collapse, introducing a reversible process under extreme conditions. This could influence models of gravitational‑wave sources, as a split would emit a distinctive burst of radiation.

However, the practical relevance remains limited because achieving near‑maximal spin requires fine‑tuned accretion histories and negligible external torques—situations that are rare in the observable universe.

## How to think about it
When evaluating exotic black‑hole scenarios, first check whether the required spin exceeds the typical values measured for astrophysical black holes (a ≲ 0.9). Next, assess the entropy balance: any proposed transformation must not decrease total entropy unless an additional energy reservoir is identified. Finally, consider observational consequences—look for sudden, symmetric bursts of gravitational waves or abrupt changes in accretion signatures that could hint at a horizon bifurcation.

<Callout type="warning">The split scenario remains purely theoretical; real black holes are unlikely to achieve the required spin, and no observations support it.</Callout>
