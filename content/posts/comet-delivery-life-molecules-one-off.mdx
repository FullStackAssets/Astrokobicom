The idea that comets delivered the building blocks of life to the early Earth has fascinated scientists for a century. A new study from the University of Cambridge, published on October 28 2026, uses a computer model to test how fast comets would hit our planet and what that means for fragile organic compounds. The researchers find that the high velocities and steep entry angles would vaporize most molecules, turning the comet‑seeded hypothesis into a rare, perhaps one‑off event. If true, this reshapes how we view the origins of prebiotic chemistry on Earth and where to look for life elsewhere.

<Callout type="takeaway">Comet‑delivered organics likely survived only a single, exceptional impact on early Earth.</Callout>

## What happened
Researchers built a digital simulation of comet trajectories intersecting Earth’s atmosphere, incorporating realistic speed distributions and entry angles measured for known comets. The model shows that typical comet speeds exceed 50 km s⁻¹, and the resulting shock heating would break apart complex organic molecules before they could reach the surface.

The study also examined Mars as a lower‑gravity alternative, finding that while some molecules might survive a Martian impact, the probability remains low. These results were published in *Scientific Reports* on October 28 2026 and challenge the long‑standing panspermia scenario that assumes a steady rain of life‑building compounds.

## Why it matters
If comets rarely delivered intact prebiotic chemistry, scientists must look to other sources—such as meteorites, volcanic synthesis, or in‑situ chemistry—to explain Earth’s early organic inventory. The finding narrows the range of viable pathways for the origin of life and influences how astrobiologists prioritize targets in the search for biosignatures on exoplanets and icy bodies.

## How to think about it
Treat comet delivery as a low‑probability, high‑impact contributor rather than a steady source. When evaluating a planetary system’s habitability, weigh the frequency of gentle impacts (e.g., carbonaceous meteorites) against the destructive potential of fast comets. Combine dynamical models with laboratory shock‑experiments to build a more complete picture of organic survival.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the model disproves all comet contributions; rare events could still have played a role in early chemistry.</Callout>
