On October 20, 2026, NASA’s Astronomy Picture of the Day featured a striking photograph of an anticrepuscular rainbow fan taken in Presenzano, Italy. The image shows a fragmented rainbow that seems to arc opposite the setting sun, creating a visual paradox. This phenomenon occurs when sunlight passes through gaps in clouds near the horizon, producing rays that converge opposite the sun’s true position. The photographer captured the scene with a phone, highlighting how everyday tools can reveal extraordinary atmospheric optics. Understanding this effect deepens our appreciation of how light and water interact in the sky.

<Callout type="takeaway">An anticrepuscular rainbow fan reveals that rainbows can appear opposite the sun when light scatters through high‑altitude clouds at sunset.</Callout>

## What happened
The picture was taken at sunset after a brief rain shower left droplets suspended in the lower atmosphere. Gaps between low‑lying clouds allowed sunlight to travel across the entire sky, intersecting the rain droplets from a direction behind the observer. This created anticrepuscular rays that converged near the horizon opposite the true solar position, producing a fan‑shaped, incomplete rainbow.

Because rainbows are formed by backward reflection of sunlight in water droplets, the sun must be behind the viewer for a conventional rainbow. In this case, the apparent location of the sun in the foreground is an optical illusion; the actual sun was setting behind the hills, hidden by clouds. The resulting fan of colors is a rare demonstration of anticrepuscular rays combined with a rainbow, a phenomenon usually seen only as faint white bands.

## Why it matters
Anticrepuscular rainbows illustrate fundamental principles of atmospheric optics, offering a vivid classroom example of light scattering, refraction, and reflection. They also remind us that everyday observations can challenge intuitive assumptions about the sky, encouraging both amateur and professional astronomers to look more closely at transient events. Public interest in such images fuels outreach, making complex physics accessible through striking visuals.

## How to think about it
When you see a rainbow that seems to point toward the sun rather than away from it, consider whether you are looking at an anticrepuscular display. Check the horizon: if the sun is hidden behind clouds or terrain, the rainbow is likely an illusion created by rays converging opposite the true solar position. Look for a fan‑shaped arc near the western horizon at sunset, and note any gaps in cloud cover that could channel sunlight across the sky. Understanding the geometry helps you distinguish this effect from a standard rainbow or halo.

<Callout type="warning">Never look directly at the sun, even during sunrise or sunset, to avoid eye damage.</Callout>
