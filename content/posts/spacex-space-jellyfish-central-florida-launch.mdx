On Tuesday morning SpaceX will attempt to launch its Bandwagon-5 rideshare mission from Cape Canaveral, targeting a one‑hour window that opens at 6:30 a.m. The Falcon 9’s first stage is on its 19th flight and will try to land on the “A Shortfall of Gravitas” droneship after delivering several small satellites and re‑entry vehicles. Observers in Central Florida may also witness a rare “space jellyfish” – a glowing, bulb‑shaped cloud formed when the rocket’s exhaust expands into sunlight‑lit upper atmosphere. The phenomenon offers both a visual treat and a reminder of the complex physics of high‑altitude exhaust plumes.

## What happened
SpaceX scheduled the Bandwagon‑5 mission for Tuesday, a rideshare flight carrying multiple small satellites and experimental re‑entry vehicles. The launch window opens at 6:30 a.m., after a previous scrub due to unfavorable recovery weather.

The Falcon 9’s first stage, on its 19th flight, will separate and attempt a controlled landing on the “A Shortfall of Gravitas” droneship stationed in the Atlantic.

When the rocket ascends into the thin upper atmosphere while the sun is still below the horizon, exhaust gases expand and become illuminated, creating the “space jellyfish” effect that was photographed during a recent launch.

## Why it matters
The visual display turns a routine orbital launch into a public outreach opportunity, sparking curiosity about spaceflight among viewers who might not otherwise follow the news. Scientists can also use the illuminated plume as a natural laboratory to study high‑altitude exhaust chemistry and its interaction with sunlight. Finally, the successful recovery of the first stage and the deployment of re‑entry vehicles demonstrate reusable‑launch economics that could lower costs for future satellite constellations.

## How to think about it
If you want to catch the “space jellyfish,” find a dark spot with an unobstructed view of the eastern horizon before sunrise. A camera with manual exposure settings will capture the faint glow better than a phone’s automatic mode. Remember that the plume appears only while the rocket is below the altitude where sunlight reaches the exhaust, so the display lasts just a few minutes.

<Callout type="warning">Do not stare directly at the bright exhaust plume for extended periods; protect your eyes as you would with any intense light source.</Callout>
