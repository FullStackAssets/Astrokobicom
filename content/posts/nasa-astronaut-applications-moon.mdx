NASA has opened its latest call for astronaut candidates as part of the Artemis program, inviting U.S. citizens with STEM or pilot backgrounds to apply by Dec 3. The announcement was made at a Kennedy Space Center air show, highlighting the agency’s push to build a sustainable lunar base and eventually send crews to Mars. With more than 8,000 hopefuls previously screened, this round seeks explorers who can live and work on the Moon, a critical step for humanity’s deep‑space ambitions.

<Callout type="takeaway">NASA’s new astronaut recruitment targets the skills needed for a permanent lunar presence and future Mars missions.</Callout>

## What happened
NASA announced the opening of applications for the next class of Artemis astronauts during a Kennedy Space Center air show. Applications close on 3 December, and the agency will make its final selection in late 2027.

Candidates must be U.S. citizens and hold at least a master’s degree in a STEM field, medical qualification, or pilot training, plus three years of relevant professional experience. Pilots need either 750 hours as pilot‑in‑command of a high‑performance jet, 1,500 total flight hours in another fixed‑wing aircraft, or 1,000 hours in a rotary‑wing aircraft.

NASA last opened its astronaut intake in 2024, selecting 10 out of more than 8,000 applicants in September 2025. Since the 1960s the agency has trained 370 astronaut candidates and currently fields 32 active astronauts.

## Why it matters
The new cohort will be essential for constructing and operating the planned lunar base, testing life‑support systems, and developing operational concepts that will feed into the 2028 crewed lunar landing and eventual Mars missions. A fresh pool of talent also sustains the commercial partnerships with SpaceX and Blue Origin, ensuring the United States retains leadership in deep‑space exploration. Moreover, the selection signals a broader shift toward long‑duration habitation beyond Earth, with implications for science, industry, and international collaboration.

## How to think about it
Prospective applicants should first verify that they meet the citizenship and education or pilot requirements, then assemble a clear record of the required flight hours or research experience. Crafting a compelling personal statement that ties one’s expertise to lunar‑base operations—such as habitat construction, EVA support, or in‑situ resource utilization—will strengthen the case. Because the selection process spans over a year, candidates can use the interim to gain additional flight time, publish relevant research, or complete advanced training to improve competitiveness.

<Callout type="warning">Do not submit an application unless you satisfy the citizenship, degree, and flight‑hour requirements; incomplete submissions will be automatically disqualified.</Callout>
