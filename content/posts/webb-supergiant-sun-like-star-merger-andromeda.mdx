Astronomers have captured the first real‑time view of a massive supergiant star swallowing a smaller, Sun‑like companion in the outskirts of the Andromeda galaxy. The event, nicknamed Bao, was first flagged by a gradual brightening in 2023, erupted in 2025, and was observed by the James Webb Space Telescope just 47 days after peak brightness. This rapid follow‑up lets scientists watch a stellar merger unfold on human timescales, something previously limited to indirect evidence. Understanding how such mergers proceed reshapes models of stellar evolution and the chemical enrichment of galaxies.

<Callout type="takeaway">Webb’s early observations prove that supergiant‑sun‑like star mergers can be caught in the act, offering a new window on stellar evolution.</Callout>

## What happened
The binary pair spent millennia in a stable orbit before the smaller star began to graze the extended envelope of the supergiant. Over the course of seven orbits—roughly seven years—the companion spiraled inward, triggering a sudden outburst that peaked in early 2025. Within a week the brightness reached its maximum, after which the two stars coalesced into a single, rejuvenated object.

James Webb’s instruments recorded the system 47 days after the outburst peak, providing the earliest‑ever spectroscopic snapshot of a merger involving a supergiant. The data revealed the pre‑merger configuration, the violent interaction phase, and the immediate post‑merger environment, allowing researchers to map temperature, composition, and dust formation in unprecedented detail.

## Why it matters
Directly witnessing a supergiant‑sun‑like merger challenges existing theories that such events unfold over centuries or millennia. The rapid timescale suggests that mass transfer can accelerate dramatically when a compact companion penetrates a puffy envelope, potentially leading to unexpected pathways toward supernovae or exotic remnants. This single case also offers a benchmark for interpreting historical transient events in distant galaxies, improving estimates of how binary interactions contribute to galactic chemical evolution.

## How to think about it
Treat the Bao merger as a prototype case when evaluating other luminous red transients. Look for the characteristic slow brightening followed by a rapid peak within a week, and prioritize early infrared follow‑up to capture dust formation. Incorporate the observed seven‑orbit timescale into binary‑star population models, but remain open to longer or shorter merger pathways depending on stellar radii and mass ratios.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume all supergiant mergers will follow the same rapid, seven‑orbit timeline; each system’s mass ratio and envelope structure can produce very different evolution.</Callout>
