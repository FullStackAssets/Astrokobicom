Tonight the Southern Taurids reach their second annual peak, promising a brief surge of bright fireballs across the November sky. As Earth slices through the debris left by comet Encke, observers can expect up to five meteors per hour that flare brighter than Venus. The display offers a rare chance for casual stargazers to witness high‑energy meteors without needing specialized equipment, and it marks the start of the Taurid fireball season that runs through December.

## What happened
Every autumn Earth passes through two streams of debris shed by the 3‑mile‑wide (4.8‑km) comet Encke, which orbits the Sun every 3.3 years. The Southern Taurids are active from Sept. 20 to Nov. 20 and the Northern Taurids from Oct. 20 to Dec. 10, each producing a radiant in the constellation Taurus.

During the peak overnight on Nov 4‑5 the Southern branch is expected to deliver up to five meteors per hour, many of them fireballs that can outshine Venus. Particles enter the atmosphere at roughly 65,000 mph (104,607 km/h) and appear to radiate from Taurus, which will be high in the southeastern sky around local midnight.

A second, similar peak from the Northern Taurids follows on Nov 11‑12, offering another chance for fireball activity under dark, near‑moonless conditions.

## Why it matters
The fireball surge provides a natural laboratory for studying the composition of cometary debris, as brighter meteors reveal spectral signatures that faint ones do not. Publicly visible events also spark interest in astronomy, encouraging backyard observing and supporting outreach programs. While the meteors pose no hazard to the surface, the heightened activity reminds us of Earth’s ongoing interaction with small Solar System bodies.

## How to think about it
Find a dark site away from city lights and face southeast after midnight. Let your eyes adapt for at least 15 minutes, then scan a wide swath of sky rather than focusing on the radiant. Bring a blanket or reclining chair, check the moon phase (the night is near‑moonless), and simply enjoy the occasional bright streaks. Remember, the goal is to take in the whole sky, not to track a single point.

<Callout type="warning">Never use a telescope or binoculars to hunt for meteors; they restrict your field of view and can distract you from the broader sky.</Callout>
