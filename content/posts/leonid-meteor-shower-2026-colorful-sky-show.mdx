The Leonid meteor shower lit up skies around the globe on the night of Nov 17‑18, 2026, delivering bright, colorful streaks that dazzled both casual observers and seasoned astrophotographers. As Earth swept through the debris trail of comet 55P/Tempel‑Tuttle, meteors burned at speeds up to 44 mi s⁻¹, creating vivid fireballs and lingering ionization trails. Photographers in Maine, Japan, and the Czech Republic captured footage of meteors that lingered for up to twelve minutes, a rarity for an annual shower. The display not only provided spectacular visuals but also offered fresh data on the structure of the Leonid stream.

<Callout type="takeaway">The 2026 Leonids delivered an especially vivid display, reminding us of the dynamic nature of Earth’s encounter with comet debris.</Callout>

## What happened
The shower reached its peak overnight on Nov 17‑18 as Earth passed through the dense portion of the Tempel‑Tuttle debris stream. Meteors entered the atmosphere at velocities of up to 44 mi s⁻¹ (71 km s⁻¹), igniting bright, often multicolored fireballs that were visible to the naked eye.

Observers reported unusually long-lasting ionization trails; one photographer in Maine recorded a bright meteor whose glowing trail persisted for twelve minutes on video, while another noted a blue‑hued trail that lingered for more than 30 seconds. The event coincided with the 60th anniversary of the historic 1966 Leonid storm, which produced up to 40 meteors per second at its peak.

## Why it matters
Beyond the visual spectacle, the 2026 Leonids provide scientists with fresh measurements of particle size distribution and stream density, improving models that predict future meteor storms. Public enthusiasm sparked by the vivid displays also drives interest in night‑sky observation and supports citizen‑science initiatives that collect observation data worldwide.

## How to think about it
Treat the Leonids as a seasonal opportunity to practice wide‑field astrophotography: use a fast lens (f/2.8 or lower), set a high ISO (1600‑3200), and keep exposures short (10‑15 seconds) to capture individual fireballs without star trails. Check local weather forecasts and moon phase charts—dark, clear skies away from city lights maximize your chances. Mobile apps that provide real‑time radiant positions can help you point your camera at the optimal part of the sky.

<Callout type="warning">Do not drive long distances in hazardous weather solely to chase meteors; prioritize personal safety over a perfect shot.</Callout>
