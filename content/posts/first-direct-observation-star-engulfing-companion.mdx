For the first time, astronomers have directly witnessed a red giant star in Andromeda swallowing its companion, a dramatic event that confirms decades of theoretical work.
The star, designated WNTR23bzdiq, is about seven times the mass of the Sun and 350 times its diameter, and it began brightening in 2018 as the companion spiraled inward.
In late 2025 the star erupted in a bright flare, and the James Webb Space Telescope captured the aftermath 47 days later, revealing the engulfment of a star about 70% the Sun’s mass.
This observation provides the first concrete evidence that dying giants can consume nearby stars, reshaping our view of binary evolution.
<Callout type="takeaway">This observation confirms that red‑giant stars can engulf nearby companions, a process long predicted but never seen directly.</Callout>
## What happened
The team first noted an increase in luminosity in 2023 using data from NEOWISE and the WINTER telescope.
By tracing archival images back to 2018, they established a steady brightening trend that suggested the red giant was growing in size.
The sudden flare at the end of 2025 was captured by JWST, which recorded a sudden spike in infrared emission and a dramatic change in the star’s spectrum.
Spectral analysis indicated that the flare was caused by a 0.7‑solar‑mass companion plunging into the giant’s envelope.
The collision produced frictional heating and ejected material equivalent to roughly 0.1–0.2 solar masses.
The event’s energy release and mass loss match predictions for common‑envelope evolution in binary systems.
## Why it matters
This finding validates models that explain how binary stars can merge or transfer mass, influencing the end stages of stellar life.
It also provides a potential pathway for producing luminous red novae and may affect the rates of certain supernovae.
Understanding these interactions helps astronomers refine population synthesis models and predict the frequency of exotic transients.

## How to think about it
When evaluating future transients, astronomers should look for gradual brightening preceding a sudden flare, especially in red giants.
Monitoring with infrared telescopes and long‑term photometric surveys increases the chance of catching the engulfment phase.
Comparing the light curve to hydrodynamic simulations can help distinguish common‑envelope events from other outbursts.
