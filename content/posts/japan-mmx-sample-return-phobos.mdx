On October 19, 2026, Japan will fire its H3 rocket carrying the Martian Moons eXploration (MMX) spacecraft toward Mars. After a ten‑month cruise, MMX will spend roughly three years orbiting Phobos, deploying the IDEFIX rover and gathering surface material. The mission plans to return the first samples from the Mars system to Earth, with a capsule slated to touch down in Australia in 2031. Success would open a new chapter in planetary science by directly probing the origins of Mars’ moons and the early solar system.
<Callout type="takeaway">MMX aims to bring the first Mars‑system samples back to Earth, reshaping our understanding of planetary formation.</Callout>
## What happened
MMX will launch from Tanegashima on an H3 rocket, then use a propulsion module to set a course for Mars. After a ten‑month journey, the spacecraft will enter Mars orbit and begin a three‑year campaign studying Phobos, including a landing of the IDEFIX rover to collect regolith.
The rover carries a suite of instruments: high‑resolution cameras, the Raman Spectrometer for MMX (RAX), a miniaturized radiometer (miniRAD), and the MEGANE gamma‑ray and neutron spectrometer developed with international partners. These tools will analyze composition, mineralogy, and temperature of Phobos’s surface.
Following sample acquisition, the return module will store the material in a sealed capsule, perform flybys of Deimos for additional observations, and depart Mars in November 2030. The capsule is scheduled to re‑enter Earth’s atmosphere and land in Australia in 2031.
## Why it matters
By delivering pristine Martian‑system material, MMX can test competing theories about whether Phobos and Deimos are captured asteroids or remnants of a giant impact. The chemical and isotopic fingerprints in the samples could also reveal conditions in the early solar system and refine models of planetary formation. International collaboration with CNES, DLR, and NASA instruments underscores a growing global partnership in deep‑space exploration.

## How to think about it
Follow MMX updates to gauge how sample‑return technologies evolve, and consider how the data might inform future missions to other small bodies. The mission also illustrates the importance of planetary‑protection protocols and international data‑sharing frameworks, which will become standard for subsequent sample‑return endeavors.
<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the returned material will be free of Earth‑origin contamination; strict planetary‑protection protocols will be applied.</Callout>
