The night sky we see with our eyes is only a fraction of the cosmic story, with most energetic events hidden in ultraviolet light. NASA has unveiled plans for UVEX, the UltraViolet EXplorer, which is scheduled to launch in 2031. Led by Caltech, the mission will perform an all‑sky survey in both near‑ and far‑ultraviolet wavelengths. By filling the ultraviolet gap, UVEX promises to reveal phenomena that current visible‑light surveys miss.

<Callout type="takeaway">UVEX will give astronomers the first complete ultraviolet map of the sky.</Callout>

## What happened
NASA confirmed that UVEX will lift off in 2031 on a dedicated launch vehicle, with Caltech serving as the mission lead. The spacecraft is designed to scan the entire celestial sphere, capturing images in two ultraviolet bands that together span the near‑ and far‑UV regimes.

The survey will complement data from the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, the Vera Rubin Observatory, and ESA’s Euclid mission, creating a multi‑wavelength picture of the universe that includes the previously under‑sampled ultraviolet domain.

## Why it matters
Ultraviolet observations are essential for studying hot, energetic processes such as stellar flares, the early stages of star formation, and the signatures of exploding stars. By providing a uniform, high‑resolution UV map, UVEX will enable researchers to cross‑reference events seen in other wavelengths, sharpening models of galaxy evolution and the life cycles of stars. The mission also opens a new window for discovering transient UV phenomena that have been invisible to ground‑based telescopes.

## How to think about it
Astronomers should begin integrating UVEX’s planned data products into their research pipelines now, treating the upcoming catalog as a baseline for future proposals. Proposal writers can design projects that pair UVEX observations with existing optical or infrared surveys to address questions about star formation rates, galaxy metallicities, or transient event rates. Data users will need to familiarize themselves with UV‑specific calibration techniques, which differ from those used for visible‑light instruments.

<Callout type="warning">The 2031 launch date is a target; budgetary or technical issues could push the timeline back, so plan contingencies accordingly.</Callout>
