At 7:48 p.m. EST on Nov. 6, a Falcon 9 roared off the pad at Vandenberg Space Force Base, painting the Pacific horizon with a sunset launch. The rocket carried the classified USSF‑432 mission for the U.S. Space Force, and its first stage touched down on the droneship “Of Course I Still Love You” after an eighth reuse. While SpaceX cut the webcast after ten minutes, the upper stage continued toward low‑Earth orbit with an unknown payload. The launch marks the 119th Falcon 9 flight of 2026 and the 14th national‑security mission this year. Observers see it as a potential addition to the secretive Starshield satellite fleet.

## What happened
SpaceX’s Falcon 9 lifted off at 7:48 p.m. EST (0048 GMT) from Vandenberg Space Force Base, using booster 1095 on its eighth flight. The first stage performed a controlled descent and landed on the droneship “Of Course I Still Love You,” completing a routine recovery.
The upper stage continued on a trajectory consistent with low‑Earth‑orbit deployments. Neither SpaceX nor the Space Force disclosed the payload, but analysts link the mission to the Starshield bus, a classified counterpart to the commercial Starlink constellation.
USSF‑432 is the 119th Falcon 9 launch of 2026 and the 14th national‑security flight, underscoring that more than 70 % of the company’s 2026 launches support the Starlink megaconstellation of over 11,000 satellites.
## Why it matters
The mission expands the U.S. Space Force’s “Space Based Sensing and Targeting” (SBST) capability, potentially adding covert LEO surveillance nodes. By leveraging SpaceX’s reusable Falcon 9, the cost per satellite drops, accelerating the deployment of a resilient, proliferated military constellation.
For the commercial sector, the launch highlights how the same launch infrastructure serves both civilian broadband and classified defense needs, blurring the line between public and secret space assets.
Strategically, the rapid cadence of national‑security flights signals a shift toward a more responsive U.S. orbital defense posture, which could influence international norms around militarized space.
