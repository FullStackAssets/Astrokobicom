On November 25, 2026, China will launch the Shenzhou‑24 mission to its Tiangong space station, and for the first time a Pakistani astronaut will fly alongside the Chinese crew. The three‑person flight marks the 18th crewed Chinese launch and the inaugural participation of a foreign payload specialist on Tiangong. This milestone follows a 2025 bilateral agreement that opened the door for Pakistan’s space agency to train astronauts in China. The week‑long stay will give Pakistan its first human spaceflight experience, edging it ahead of neighboring India’s crewed plans. The mission also signals China’s push to broaden international partnerships as it prepares for a lunar research station.

<Callout type="takeaway">A Pakistani astronaut will become the first foreign crew member aboard China’s Tiangong space station.</Callout>

## What happened
China’s Shenzhou‑24 will lift off on November 25, 2026 atop a Long March 2F rocket, carrying three taikonauts to rendezvous with the Tianhe core module of Tiangong. While the Chinese commander and pilot will be announced the day before launch, one seat is reserved for a Pakistani payload specialist—either Muhammad Zeeshan Ali or Khurram Daud—making it the first joint flight of Chinese and foreign astronauts.

The partnership began with a February 2025 agreement between the China Manned Space Agency and Pakistan’s SUPARCO. After a multi‑stage selection process that concluded in April 2026, the two Pakistani candidates completed training in China; one will fly, and the other will serve as backup. After a roughly one‑week stay aboard Tiangong, the Pakistani astronaut will return to Earth aboard the Shenzhou‑23 spacecraft.

## Why it matters
The flight showcases China’s willingness to open its orbital platform to non‑Chinese partners, a step toward the collaborative model seen at the International Space Station. For Pakistan, the mission delivers its first human spaceflight experience, boosting national prestige and scientific capacity while narrowing the gap with India’s upcoming crewed effort. Regionally, the partnership may intensify competition but also demonstrates how emerging space nations can leverage established programs to accelerate their own ambitions.

## How to think about it
When assessing new international space collaborations, weigh the political motivations, technology‑transfer benefits, and the long‑term goals of each partner. A short‑term crew swap may boost prestige, but sustainable cooperation requires joint research agendas, shared infrastructure, and clear exit strategies.
