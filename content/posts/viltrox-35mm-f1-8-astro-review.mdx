The new Viltrox AF 35mm f/1.8 II EVO FE (APO) lens hit the market with a sub‑$400 price tag, promising a fast aperture in a compact package. Space.com’s hands‑on review puts the lens through its paces on a Sony FE‑mount body, focusing on star‑field performance. While the central image quality holds up surprisingly well, the edges reveal a distinct astigmatism when shooting wide open. Understanding these trade‑offs matters for anyone building a low‑cost astrophotography kit.

<Callout type="takeaway">The Viltrox 35mm f/1.8 delivers sharp central stars at a budget price, but edge astigmatism limits its wide‑open performance.</Callout>

## What happened
The reviewers tested the lens at its native f/1.8 aperture and found the center of the frame to be crisp, with star points resolving cleanly even in short exposures. However, at the left and right edges the stars stretched into tiny crosses, a classic sign of astigmatism that only becomes visible when examined at 100%.

When stopped down to the sweet spot between f/8 and f/11, the entire image – including the corners – became uniformly sharp, and chromatic aberration was minimal thanks to the apochromatic (APO) optical design. The lens comprises 13 elements in 10 groups, a fairly complex formula for its price range, and it is offered for both Nikon Z and Sony FE mounts.

## Why it matters
For hobbyists and newcomers, the price point removes a major barrier to entry for serious night‑sky work. A fast 35mm focal length is versatile for both wide Milky Way shots and tighter deep‑sky targets, and the APO coating helps keep colour fringing under control. The downside—edge astigmatism at f/1.8—means users must either accept slightly softer corners or stop down, which can increase exposure times and affect sky background noise. Knowing these limits lets photographers plan exposure strategies and decide whether the savings outweigh the performance compromise.

## How to think about it
Treat the Viltrox 35mm as a two‑step tool: shoot at f/1.8 for quick framing and light‑gathering, then stop down to f/8‑f/11 for the final stacked exposure to capture the cleanest stars across the frame. Pair the lens with a sturdy equatorial mount and use dithering or stacking software to mitigate any residual corner softness. If you primarily shoot Milky Way panoramas where the center dominates the composition, the lens can be a cost‑effective workhorse.

<Callout type="warning">Do not rely on the lens at f/1.8 for critical star shape work; stop down to at least f/8 for uniform sharpness.</Callout>
