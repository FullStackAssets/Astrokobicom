The Moon is not a static rock; new research shows it is slowly contracting. By combing through more than 10,000 fresh faults captured by NASA’s Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter Camera, scientists have quantified the shrinkage. Their calculations indicate the lunar radius is about 45 meters smaller than it was 400‑800 million years ago. This subtle change reshapes our understanding of lunar tectonics and the thermal evolution of rocky bodies.

<Callout type="takeaway">The Moon’s radius has decreased by roughly 45 meters over the last several hundred million years.</Callout>

## What happened
In a recent analysis of LROC imagery, researchers identified over 10,000 young faults—lobate scarps, mare ridges, and other tension cracks—scattered across the lunar highlands and maria. These features are the surface expression of the Moon’s interior cooling and consequent contraction.

By measuring the typical offset across the scarps and integrating the global distribution, the team estimated a net reduction in the Moon’s radius of about 148 feet (45 meters) over a span of 400 to 800 million years. The Moon’s single‑plate structure means these faults appear anywhere, unlike Earth’s plate‑boundary earthquakes.

## Why it matters
Understanding that the Moon is actively shrinking refines models of its thermal history and internal dynamics, which are crucial for interpreting surface geology and planning future habitats. A quantified contraction rate helps engineers anticipate subtle surface deformations that could affect long‑term infrastructure, such as bases or telescopes, and offers a comparative benchmark for other airless worlds that undergo similar cooling.

## How to think about it
When evaluating lunar sites, factor the measured contraction into long‑term stability models, especially in regions dense with lobate scarps. Educators can use the Moon’s shrinkage as a case study of planetary cooling, illustrating how interior processes manifest on the surface. Mission planners should treat the Moon as a dynamic, single‑plate body rather than a static platform.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the lunar surface is uniformly stable; localized fault activity can still pose hazards for precise operations.</Callout>
