On October 19, 2026, Japan’s Martian Moons Exploration (MMX) spacecraft blasted off from Tanegashima aboard an H3 rocket, beginning a five‑year quest to retrieve material from Phobos, the larger of Mars’s two moons. The mission will enter orbit around Phobos, deploy a lander, and collect soil and pebble samples in a world‑first sample‑return from a Martian moon. By delivering these fragments to Earth, scientists hope to finally decide whether the moons are captured asteroids or debris from an ancient impact. The launch marks the most ambitious international effort to study Mars’s satellite system, involving JAXA, NASA, ESA, France and Germany. Success could reshape our understanding of planetary formation across the solar system.

<Callout type="takeaway">MMX will be the first mission to bring back samples from a Martian moon, potentially solving the mystery of Phobos’s origin.</Callout>

## What happened

The MMX spacecraft lifted off on Monday, October 19 at 20:41 BST from the Tanegashima Space Center on an H3 launch vehicle. Over the next several months it will cruise toward Mars, enter orbit around Phobos, and conduct detailed mapping before releasing a small lander.

Once on Phobos, the lander will perform a brief touch‑and‑go maneuver, using a pneumatic drill and a specialized sampling device to gather regolith and small pebbles in the moon’s ultra‑low gravity—about 0.1 % of Earth’s. The sealed sample container will then be lifted back to the orbiter for a return journey to Earth, slated for 2031.

## Why it matters

Retrieving material from Phobos offers a direct test of competing theories about the moon’s origin—whether it is a captured asteroid or the result of a massive impact on Mars. Chemical and isotopic analyses of the samples could reveal a composition that matches Martian crust or typical asteroid mineralogy, settling a decades‑long debate. Moreover, the mission demonstrates new low‑gravity sampling techniques that will be essential for future asteroid and small‑body missions, expanding humanity’s ability to study the building blocks of the solar system.
