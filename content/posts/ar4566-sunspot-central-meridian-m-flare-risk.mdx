A massive sunspot region designated AR4566 has just crossed the Sun’s central meridian, placing it directly in Earth’s line of sight. The region has already produced 18 of the 19 C‑class flares recorded this period, including a near‑M‑class C9.3 event. Forecasters now assign a 60 % chance of an M‑class flare and a 15 % chance of an X‑class flare today. While no Earth‑directed coronal mass ejection has been seen yet, the positioning makes any eruption especially geoeffective. Scientists and satellite operators are watching closely.

<Callout type="takeaway">AR4566’s central‑meridian position puts Earth at heightened risk of an M‑class solar flare.</Callout>

## What happened
On November 10, AR4566 moved onto the Sun’s central meridian, the imaginary line that divides the Earth‑facing side. The region’s magnetic complexity (beta‑gamma‑delta) kept it highly active, generating 18 C‑class flares, the strongest being a C9.3 at 08:34 UTC.

The NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center gave AR4566 a 60 % probability of an M‑class flare and a 15 % probability of an X‑class flare for the day. Coronagraph images from SOHO showed no Earth‑directed CME so far, and solar‑wind speeds have eased from high to moderate levels.

## Why it matters
A flare of M‑class intensity can disrupt high‑frequency radio communications, degrade satellite navigation, and trigger beautiful but potentially hazardous auroras at lower latitudes. Power‑grid operators and astronauts in low‑Earth orbit must prepare for increased radiation exposure, making early warning essential for mitigating economic and safety impacts.

## How to think about it
Treat the forecast as a probability, not a certainty. Keep an eye on NOAA and NASA alerts, especially during the next 24‑48 hours. If you rely on satellite‑based services, consider backup communication channels. For power‑grid operators, review contingency plans for geomagnetic induced currents. Astronauts should follow agency radiation‑exposure guidelines and limit EVA time if alerts rise.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that a forecasted flare will definitely launch an Earth‑directed CME; the situation remains uncertain.</Callout>
