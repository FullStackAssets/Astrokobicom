Supermassive black holes that gulp gas at high rates were thought to form cold, flat accretion disks that suppress jet formation. Yet the galaxy 3C 120 in Taurus defies this expectation, showing a bright, relativistic jet despite rapid feeding. A new study combining XRISM X‑ray spectroscopy with global radio and millimeter arrays has identified a hot plasma flow that links the inner disk to the jet launch point. This discovery offers a concrete mechanism for how powerful jets can arise in rapidly accreting systems.

<Callout type="takeaway">Hot plasma extending from the inner disk provides the missing link that powers jets in fast‑feeding black holes.</Callout>

## What happened
XRISM detected a broadened iron emission line from the accretion disk of 3C 120. The relativistic distortion of the line shows that the cold, relatively flat disk truncates at roughly 20 gravitational radii from the black hole, beyond which the flow becomes hot and tenuous.

Simultaneous observations with the Global Millimeter VLBI Array, the Very Long Baseline Array, and the East Asian VLBI Network traced the narrowing jet back to its origin inside this hot plasma region. The hot flow reaches temperatures of hundreds of millions of degrees, providing the pressure and magnetic conditions needed to launch a relativistic jet.

The findings, published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters, represent the first direct, multi‑wavelength view of a hot inner flow feeding a jet in a rapidly accreting supermassive black hole.

## Why it matters
Understanding how jets form in actively feeding black holes resolves a key tension between accretion theory and observed radio‑loud active galaxies. Jets regulate galaxy growth by injecting energy into their surroundings, so pinpointing their origin improves models of galaxy evolution and black‑hole feedback. The hot‑plasma mechanism also offers a unified explanation for why some high‑accretion systems are jet‑quiet while others, like 3C 120, remain powerful jet producers.

## How to think about it
When evaluating jet‑producing active galaxies, consider the state of the inner accretion flow as a potential jet engine. If the cold disk truncates far enough from the event horizon, look for signatures of a hot, radiatively inefficient plasma—broad iron lines, high‑energy continua, and compact radio cores. Incorporate these observational markers into simulations to test whether magnetic fields anchored in the hot flow can extract rotational energy and drive relativistic outflows.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that all rapidly accreting black holes will launch jets; the presence of a hot inner flow appears necessary but not sufficient without the right magnetic configuration.</Callout>
