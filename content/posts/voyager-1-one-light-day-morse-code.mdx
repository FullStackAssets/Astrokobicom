On November 17, 2026, astronomy enthusiasts gathered under the night sky to flash a Morse‑code message—V‑G‑R—toward the distant Voyager 1 spacecraft. The brief on‑off beam was captured by the Emirates Astronomical Observatory in a long exposure, turning a simple flashlight into a cosmic telegram. The signal took exactly one day to reach Voyager 1, which celebrated its one‑light‑day distance from Earth on November 18 at 10:16:07 UTC. This milestone underscores how far humanity’s first interstellar probe has traveled, now over 26 billion kilometres away. It also invites the public to contemplate the vast scales of space and the limits of real‑time communication.
<Callout type="takeaway">Voyager 1’s one‑light‑day distance shows our messages now need a full day to reach humanity’s most distant spacecraft.</Callout>
## What happened
Fans used a handheld flashlight to flash the letters V, G, and R in Morse code, creating a series of dashes and dots that were recorded by a time‑exposure image at the Emirates Astronomical Observatory. The resulting photograph, featured as NASA’s Astronomy Picture of the Day, displays the luminous pattern against the night sky, effectively sending a symbolic call sign to Voyager 1.
Voyager 1, launched in 1977, passed the one‑light‑day distance from Earth on 18 November 2026 at 10:16:07 UTC. At that moment the probe was roughly 26 billion kilometres away, traveling through interstellar space beyond the heliosphere.
At its current speed, the spacecraft will not reach a full light‑year (365 light‑days) from Earth for about 18,250 years, highlighting the immense timescales of interstellar travel.
## Why it matters
Reaching a one‑light‑day separation is a tangible reminder that any signal sent from Earth now takes a full day to arrive at Voyager 1, limiting real‑time control and emphasizing the challenges of deep‑space communication. The public‑driven Morse‑code event also demonstrates how citizen participation can celebrate scientific milestones, fostering broader interest in space exploration. Moreover, the milestone provides a reference point for future missions that may venture even farther, informing engineering designs for long‑delay communication systems.

## How to think about it
When considering messages to distant probes, treat the light‑time delay as a fixed latency: calculate the round‑trip time, plan commands well in advance, and use automated onboard systems for real‑time decisions. Use the one‑light‑day milestone as a teaching example to illustrate exponential distances and the difference between near‑Earth and interstellar missions.
<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that flashing lights or other casual signals can modify Voyager 1’s operations or data collection.</Callout>
