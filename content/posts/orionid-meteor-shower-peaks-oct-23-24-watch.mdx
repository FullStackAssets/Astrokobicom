The Orionid meteor shower, one of the two major streams tied to Halley's Comet, reaches its peak this week. Observers in both hemispheres can expect up to 20 meteors per hour after midnight on Wednesday night into Thursday morning. A bright waxing moon will linger low in the sky, dimming the display until it sets. Because the Orionids are fast, bright, and can leave glowing trains, they offer a spectacular show for casual skywatchers. Knowing when and where to look maximizes your chances of catching these fleeting fireballs.

## What happened
The Orionid meteor shower is produced by debris left behind by Halley's Comet, which passes near Earth roughly every 75 years. As Earth sweeps through this stream each October, tiny particles enter the atmosphere at high speed, heating up and burning away as bright meteors.

During the peak on Wednesday night into Thursday morning (Oct 23‑24), the American Meteor Society estimates observers can see up to 20 meteors per hour under ideal dark‑sky conditions. The shower is visible across the sky, not just in the Orion constellation, and can be observed from both the Northern and Southern hemispheres after midnight.

## Why it matters
The Orionids provide a rare, public‑friendly opportunity to witness fast, bright fireballs that can leave glowing trains lasting several seconds. Such displays spark interest in astronomy, encourage outdoor activity, and remind us of Earth’s ongoing interaction with cometary debris. For scientists, consistent meteor rates help refine models of the Halley stream and atmospheric entry processes.

## How to think about it
Choose a viewing site far from city lights and give your eyes 15‑20 minutes to adapt to the darkness. Aim to watch after midnight, when the moon has set and the sky is darkest. Bring a reclining chair or blanket, dress warmly, and scan the sky rather than focusing on a single point. Keep a casual, patient mindset; meteors appear randomly, so occasional pauses are normal.

<Callout type="warning">Do not attempt to view the shower with binoculars or telescopes pointed directly at the sky, and never look at the sun while searching for meteors.</Callout>
