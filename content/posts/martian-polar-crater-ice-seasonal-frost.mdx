Mars’s northern polar region experiences dramatic seasonal shifts, but the details of how ice behaves inside its craters have remained unclear. An international team examined high‑resolution images of 36 craters captured by the HiRISE camera between 2006 and 2025 (Mars Years 28–38). They found that 32 craters showed bright‑deposit changes that correspond to ice expanding in winter and retreating in summer. This pattern mirrors Earth’s seasonal frost cycles yet occurs on a planet with a thin atmosphere and extreme temperature swings. Understanding these dynamics helps scientists reconstruct Mars’s climate history and assess its habitability potential.

<Callout type="takeaway">Seasonal frost causes measurable expansion and contraction of ice in most Martian polar craters.</Callout>

## What happened
The researchers used HiRISE imagery to track brightness variations on crater floors, interpreting brighter areas as surface frost or ice. Across the 36‑crater sample, 32 displayed clear seasonal brightening and darkening, indicating ice deposits grow during the cold season and recede when temperatures rise. The remaining four craters showed no detectable change, suggesting local factors such as crater geometry or dust cover may inhibit frost formation.

These observations span nearly two decades of Martian time, providing a longitudinal view of how polar ice responds to the planet’s 687‑day orbit. The study confirms that seasonal frost is not limited to broad ice cap margins but actively reshapes smaller topographic features.

## Why it matters
Seasonal ice movement in craters offers a proxy for short‑term climate variability on Mars, complementing long‑term ice cap studies. By linking frost cycles to specific locations, researchers can refine models of atmospheric water transport and surface‑subsurface exchange. This insight improves predictions of where subsurface water might be stable, a key factor for future exploration and potential in‑situ resource utilization.

Moreover, the variability observed across craters highlights the importance of micro‑climates in shaping Martian geology, informing where to target landing sites for climate‑focused missions.

## How to think about it
When evaluating Martian climate data, treat crater frost cycles as micro‑indicators that complement regional ice cap measurements. Compare seasonal brightness trends with atmospheric models to isolate the role of local topography. For mission planning, prioritize craters that show consistent frost cycles, as they may host near‑surface ice accessible to landers.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume that all polar craters behave identically; variations in dust cover, slope, and latitude can suppress observable frost signatures.</Callout>
