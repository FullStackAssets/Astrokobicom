When you glance at the night sky in Perseus, a faint flicker may catch your eye. That flicker belongs to Algol, a star whose brightness drops and rises like a cosmic heartbeat. Modern astronomers have measured its cycle at precisely 2 days 20 hours 49 minutes, and its light output swings by a factor of about three. This regular dimming earned ancient observers the chilling moniker "Demon Star," linking astronomy with myth. Understanding Algol’s behavior reveals the mechanics of eclipsing binaries and the cultural roots of star naming.

<Callout type="takeaway">Algol’s predictable dimming every ~2.9 days turned a celestial oddity into a mythic "Demon Star."</Callout>

## What happened
Algol is an eclipsing binary system composed of a bright primary star and a dimmer companion that pass in front of each other every 2 days 20 hours 49 minutes. When the secondary star blocks the primary, the system’s apparent magnitude drops from about 2.1 to 3.4, roughly a three‑fold decrease in brightness. The pair is part of a wider triple system, with a third star orbiting the inner binary over a longer period, making Algol a classic laboratory for stellar dynamics.

The star’s variability was noted by ancient Arab astronomers, who called it al‑Ghul, meaning “the demon’s head.” Early Greek sky‑watchers linked the alternating light to the myth of Medusa’s winking eye, dubbing it the “Demon Star.” Modern observations confirm that the regular eclipses are the source of the legend.

## Why it matters
Algol provides a textbook case for studying stellar masses, radii, and orbital mechanics because its eclipses are regular and observable with modest equipment. By modeling its light curve, astronomers refine theories of stellar evolution and binary interaction. Culturally, the star illustrates how human societies have woven celestial phenomena into myth, reminding us that scientific interpretation often builds on ancient storytelling.

## How to think about it
To observe Algol’s cycle, start by locating the constellation Perseus and identifying Algol’s bright neighbor Almach. Note Algol’s brightness on a clear night, then check again after roughly 24 hours; you should see a slight dimming. Repeating this over several nights will reveal the full 2‑day‑20‑hour‑49‑minute pattern. Comparing its magnitude to nearby stars such as Epsilon Persei at minimum and Almach at maximum provides a simple visual gauge without needing photometric equipment.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume Algol’s dimming indicates any danger; it is a normal stellar eclipse with no effect on Earth.</Callout>
