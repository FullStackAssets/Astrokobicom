On 21 January 1974, Skylab astronaut Edward Gibson watched a solar flare erupt and recorded it from inception to decay. Using the Apollo Telescope Mount, he captured a continuous sequence of images, the first ever of a complete flare. The data revealed how flare brightness and structure evolve, filling a critical gap in solar physics. This achievement linked human spaceflight with real‑time solar monitoring, a practice that underpins today’s space‑weather forecasting.

<Callout type="takeaway">Gibson’s live capture of a solar flare proved that astronauts could directly study dynamic solar events from orbit.</Callout>

## What happened
Skylab’s crew—Gerald Carr, Edward Gibson, and William Pogue—operated the Apollo Telescope Mount, a suite of eight instruments that imaged the Sun in X‑ray, ultraviolet and visible light. During the mission they collected roughly 75,000 solar images, establishing the first extensive space‑based solar archive.

On 21 January 1974 Gibson, a trained solar physicist, kept the telescope trained on an active region. As the flare began, the instruments tracked its rise, peak, and gradual fade, producing a full temporal record that had never been seen before. Scientists later used the sequence to identify the timing of energy release and plasma heating.

On 16 November 2026 the Sun fired ten modest flares—five C‑class and five B‑class—with the strongest reaching C5.4. While none match the historic significance of Gibson’s 1974 capture, they illustrate the Sun’s ongoing variability and the continued relevance of space‑based observations.

## Why it matters
The uninterrupted flare record gave researchers a concrete example of how magnetic energy converts to radiation, informing models that predict solar storms. Accurate forecasts protect satellites, power grids, and astronauts from harmful radiation. Moreover, the episode showed that crew‑operated instruments could supplement ground‑based monitoring, a concept revived in modern missions such as the ISS Solar Array.

## How to think about it
When evaluating historic solar observations, treat them as qualitative benchmarks rather than precise measurements. Combine them with modern multi‑wavelength data to trace long‑term trends. For educators, use Gibson’s story to illustrate how human presence in space can enhance scientific return, encouraging students to consider interdisciplinary roles in future missions.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume early flare images have the spatial resolution of modern solar telescopes; they serve as qualitative benchmarks.</Callout>
