On November 7, 1996, NASA lifted off the Mars Global Surveyor (MGS), its first dedicated Mars orbiter in nearly two decades. The spacecraft entered Martian orbit to map the entire planet and act as a communications relay for surface rovers. Over nine years it returned more than 240,000 images and uncovered evidence of water‑related minerals and climate change at the south pole. Its data guided the landing sites of the Spirit and Opportunity rovers, reshaping our understanding of Mars. The mission ended in November 2006 after a battery was inadvertently exposed to sunlight, cutting power permanently.

<Callout type="takeaway">The Mars Global Surveyor reignited Mars exploration, delivering critical data that shaped later rover missions.</Callout>

## What happened
The Mars Global Surveyor was launched aboard a Delta II rocket and became the first successful NASA Mars mission since the Viking landers of 1975. As an orbiter, it mapped the Martian surface, atmosphere, and interior while also relaying data from the Sojourner, Spirit, and Opportunity rovers back to Earth.

MGS discovered surface channels that appeared to have been carved by flowing water, identified hematite deposits—an iron oxide that forms in watery environments—and observed changes at the south polar ice cap suggesting climate variability over time.

Designed for a nominal mission life of one year, MGS far outlasted expectations, operating for nine active years before a maneuver exposed one of its batteries to direct sunlight, causing a loss of power that silenced the probe in November 2006.

## Why it matters
MGS provided the most comprehensive global map of Mars at the time, enabling scientists to pinpoint promising sites for future landers and rovers. Its detection of water‑related minerals directly influenced the selection of Spirit and Opportunity landing zones, leading to the landmark discovery of ancient aqueous environments. The mission also demonstrated the value of an orbital communications relay, a capability that remains essential for current and upcoming Mars missions.

## How to think about it
When evaluating Mars research, treat MGS data as the foundational layer that other missions build upon. Use its global maps to contextualize localized rover findings, and remember that its water‑related observations were the first clues that prompted more detailed investigations by later landers. For anyone studying Martian climate change, compare the south‑pole observations from MGS with newer orbital datasets to track long‑term trends.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume MGS observations alone prove that liquid water currently flows on Mars; later missions are needed for confirmation.</Callout>
