On April 1, a bright bolide streaked across the South Atlantic, lighting up the sky at an altitude of nearly 60 miles (90.5 km). The flash was captured simultaneously by U.S. military and civil space‑based sensors and by the civilian GOES‑E and Europe’s Meteosat‑3 Gen (MTG) Imager‑1 geostationary satellites. Similar multi‑sensor observations have recorded an April 24, 2025 fireball over Alaska with a dense seismo‑acoustic network, and a May 20, 2023 event over Queensland that released energy comparable to a 7.1‑kiloton bomb. Together these detections illustrate how coordinated satellite monitoring turns brief fireballs into valuable data for planetary‑defense planning.

## What happened

On April 1, a bolide entered the atmosphere over the South Atlantic, reaching peak brightness at about 90.5 km altitude. U.S. government space‑based sensors recorded the event, and the same flash was seen by NOAA’s GOES‑E and Europe’s MTG Imager‑1 using their lightning imagers.

A second case occurred on April 24, 2025 over south‑central Alaska. A network of 37 seismic stations, 16 single infrasound sensors and four infrasound arrays captured 30 ballistic and fragmentation arrivals, providing one of the most detailed seismo‑acoustic records of a natural bolide.

The third notable fireball struck on May 20, 2023 above northwest Queensland, Australia. The 3.7‑m rock fragmented catastrophically, releasing energy equivalent to a 7.1‑kiloton explosion, and was logged as one of the 20 most energetic events in the CNEOS database since 1988.

## Why it matters

These observations give scientists a three‑dimensional view of entry dynamics, fragmentation behavior, and energy release, all of which are essential for accurate impact modeling. By linking satellite detections with ground‑based seismic and infrasound data, researchers can validate trajectory reconstructions and improve predictions of where meteorites may land. The ability to rapidly assess the size and strength of incoming objects also informs emergency managers about potential air‑burst hazards, reducing the risk of surprise damage.

## How to think about it

Treat each satellite alert as the first layer of a multi‑tiered verification process. Pair the initial light‑curve data with seismic, infrasound, and radar records to refine trajectory and energy estimates. Prioritize events that exceed a few kilotons of TNT equivalent for rapid impact‑risk assessment, and feed the combined dataset into simulation tools that model atmospheric breakup and ground footprint.

<Callout type="warning">Do not rely on a single satellite detection to gauge impact risk; always corroborate with independent ground‑based observations.</Callout>
