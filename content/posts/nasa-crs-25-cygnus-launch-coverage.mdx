NASA has set the schedule for the next commercial resupply flight to the International Space Station, targeting a launch no earlier than 10:47 a.m. EST on Sunday, Nov. 29. The Northrop Grumman Cygnus XL, named S.S. James “Jim” A. Lovell, will ride atop a SpaceX Falcon 9 from Cape Canaveral’s SLC‑40. A pre‑launch media teleconference will be streamed on Nov. 23, giving scientists and the public a chance to hear directly from NASA, Northrop Grumman, and SpaceX officials. The mission will deliver roughly 11,000 pounds of science investigations, supplies, and hardware to the orbiting laboratory. Live coverage of both the launch and the subsequent docking will be available across NASA’s digital platforms.

<Callout type="takeaway">The CRS‑25 launch will replenish the ISS while showcasing a multi‑partner commercial resupply model.</Callout>

## What happened
NASA announced that the Cygnus XL spacecraft, built by Northrop Grumman, is slated to launch on a Falcon 9 booster provided by SpaceX. The vehicle will lift off from Space Launch Complex 40 at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station and carry about 11,000 pounds of cargo, including experiments aimed at improving drug delivery, cancer diagnostics, muscle‑loss mitigation, radiation sensing, and food production for long‑duration missions.

After launch, astronauts Jessica Watkins and Luke Delaney will use the station’s Canadarm2 to capture the free‑flying Cygnus on Dec. 1 and berth it to the Unity module’s Earth‑facing port. The spacecraft will remain attached until May 2027, when it will be filled with disposal items and de‑orbit, burning up harmlessly in the atmosphere.

## Why it matters
Resupplying the ISS is essential for maintaining continuous scientific output and crew health, and the CRS‑25 flight extends that capability through 2027. By delivering cutting‑edge biomedical and life‑support experiments, the mission supports research that could translate to better treatments on Earth and enable future deep‑space habitats. The partnership among NASA, Northrop Grumman, and SpaceX also demonstrates the viability of commercial providers handling routine logistics, reducing costs for the agency.

## How to think about it
If you want to follow the mission, mark the Nov. 29 launch window on your calendar and check NASA’s livestream schedule on the agency’s website or YouTube channel. Use the same resources on Dec. 1 to watch the robotic capture and berthing, which offers a clear view of the Canadarm2 in action. For educators, the experiment payload list can be a springboard for classroom projects that connect ISS research to everyday health challenges.

<Callout type="warning">Launch times are provisional; weather or technical issues can push the liftoff later than the announced 10:47 a.m. EST window.</Callout>
