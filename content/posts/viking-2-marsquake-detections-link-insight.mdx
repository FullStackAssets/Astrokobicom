Back in 1976 the Viking 2 lander recorded two brief tremors on Mars, a finding that was dismissed as ambiguous for decades. Recent work re‑examines those signals alongside the flood of data from NASA’s InSight lander, which logged almost a thousand marsquakes between 2018 and 2022. By matching the locations of the old and new events, scientists are piecing together a longer seismic history of the Red Planet. This connection reshapes our view of Martian tectonics and informs future mission designs. Understanding whether Mars remains geologically active is crucial for assessing its habitability and resource potential.

<Callout type="takeaway">Viking 2’s tentative 1976 marsquakes line up with InSight’s modern detections, suggesting Mars has been seismically active for at least four decades.</Callout>

## What happened
The Viking 2 lander touched down in Utopia Planitia on 3 September 1976, carrying a seismometer mounted on its deck. The instrument registered two short‑duration events on sol 53 and sol 80, which researchers at the time interpreted as possible marsquakes but could not rule out wind‑induced vibrations.

From 2018 to 2022 NASA’s InSight lander, positioned in nearby Elysium Planitia, recorded nearly 1,000 marsquakes, providing the most comprehensive seismic catalog for Mars to date. A comparative study published in *AGU Advances* on 30 September 2026 re‑analyzed the Viking 2 signals and found that the two 1976 events originated from a region overlapping InSight’s most active seismic zone.

Because Utopia and Elysium Planitia lie within the broader Elysium‑Utopia basin, the spatial coincidence supports the idea that the same subsurface structures have been generating tremors for decades.

## Why it matters
Linking Viking 2’s early detections to InSight’s modern catalog extends Mars’ seismic record by more than forty years, offering a rare long‑term view of planetary tectonics. This continuity strengthens the case that Mars retains internal heat and active faulting, factors that influence atmospheric evolution and potential subsurface habitats. It also provides a benchmark for designing future seismometers that can distinguish genuine quakes from environmental noise.

## How to think about it
When evaluating historic seismic data, start by assessing the instrument’s coupling to the ground and its susceptibility to environmental noise. Compare event timing and waveform characteristics with modern, better‑characterized datasets to look for spatial and temporal patterns. Treat early detections as clues rather than definitive measurements, and incorporate their uncertainties into broader models of Martian geologic activity.

<Callout type="warning">Do not treat the Viking 2 detections as confirmed earthquakes; the data remain vulnerable to wind‑induced noise and limited sensor coupling.</Callout>
