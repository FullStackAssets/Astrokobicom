Voyager 1 crossed the one‑light‑day threshold on 18 November 2026, placing it roughly 26 billion kilometres (16 billion miles) from Earth. The probe, launched in 1977, has spent nearly five decades journeying through the solar system and now operates in interstellar space. One light‑day is the distance light travels in 24 hours, a stark illustration of the vastness of the region beyond the Sun. This milestone highlights both the engineering durability of Voyager and the sheer scale of distances we aim to traverse. Scientists will continue to rely on its instruments to study the tenuous material that fills interstellar space.

## What happened
Voyager 1, launched in 1977, is now about 26 billion kilometres from Earth, the distance a photon covers in a single day. The spacecraft’s radio link takes roughly 24 hours to reach it, and another 24 hours for a reply, making real‑time communication impossible.

The probe still carries a 12‑inch gold‑plated "Golden Record" intended for any extraterrestrials that might encounter it. While its cameras have been turned off to conserve power, Voyager 1 continues to measure magnetic fields, cosmic rays, and plasma density, providing a unique window into the interstellar medium.

NASA’s team monitors the spacecraft’s dwindling power budget, periodically shutting down non‑essential systems. Yet, even with limited resources, the probe remains a valuable scientific outpost beyond the heliosphere.

## Why it matters
Reaching a light‑day distance underscores the enormity of interstellar space and the challenges future missions will face. Voyager 1’s longevity offers a proof‑of‑concept for long‑duration engineering, informing designs for next‑generation probes that may travel farther. The data it streams about magnetic fields and particle environments help refine models of how the Sun interacts with the galaxy, a key factor for protecting future crewed missions.

Moreover, the milestone captures public imagination, reminding us that humanity’s reach extends far beyond the planets we once thought were the edge of exploration. It also provides a baseline for measuring how the interstellar medium changes over decades.

## How to think about it
Treat Voyager 1’s light‑day achievement as a scale marker rather than a destination. When evaluating future interstellar concepts, compare required travel time, power budgets, and communication latency against this benchmark. Use the probe’s data to calibrate models of the heliopause and to anticipate the radiation environment that any crewed vessel would encounter beyond the solar wind.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume Voyager 1 can provide real‑time data; the round‑trip signal takes about 48 hours and bandwidth is extremely limited.</Callout>
