A new analysis from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) collaboration has tightened the Milky Way’s mass measurement to an unprecedented level. By combining over 10,000 distant stars observed by DESI with proper‑motion data from Gaia, the team derived a total mass of 1.19 trillion solar masses. The reported uncertainty is roughly 11 percent, a dramatic improvement over earlier, widely divergent estimates. Understanding the Galaxy’s mass is crucial for mapping dark‑matter distribution and for calibrating models of galaxy formation.

<Callout type="takeaway">DESI now pins the Milky Way’s mass at 1.19 trillion suns with only ~11 % uncertainty.</Callout>

## What happened
The DESI team selected more than 10,000 halo stars located between about 30,000 and 310,000 light‑years from the Galactic centre. These stars belong to two well‑understood aging populations whose intrinsic brightness serves as reliable distance markers. Four independent dynamical modeling approaches were applied to each population, and each method was first validated on mock surveys that mimic DESI’s observational footprint.

Proper‑motion measurements from the Gaia mission were cross‑matched to the DESI spectroscopic data, providing three‑dimensional velocity information for the same stars. By fitting the combined position‑velocity dataset with the four models and averaging the results, the collaboration arrived at a best‑fit total mass of 1.19 × 10^12 M☉ with an uncertainty of about 11 percent.

## Why it matters
A precise Galactic mass anchors the scale of the Milky Way’s dark‑matter halo, influencing everything from satellite galaxy dynamics to the interpretation of stellar streams. The new figure narrows the range of viable dark‑matter density profiles, helping to test competing theories of dark‑matter physics. It also provides a firmer baseline for cosmological simulations that aim to reproduce the formation of Milky‑like galaxies.

## How to think about it
Treat the 1.19 trillion‑solar‑mass figure as the new reference point for any work that requires the Milky Way’s total mass, but keep the 11 % margin in mind when drawing quantitative conclusions. When building dynamical models or running cosmological simulations, incorporate this value as a prior and explore the allowed range to gauge sensitivity. For observers, the result highlights the value of combining spectroscopic surveys with precise astrometry, suggesting that future joint analyses (e.g., with Rubin Observatory data) could push uncertainties even lower.

<Callout type="warning">Do not assume the 11 % uncertainty accounts for all systematic errors; model choices and halo‑shape assumptions can introduce additional biases.</Callout>
