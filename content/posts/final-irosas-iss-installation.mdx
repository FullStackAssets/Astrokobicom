The International Space Station received its eighth and final iROSA solar array on 5 November 2026. NASA astronaut Anil Menon and CSA astronaut Joshua Kutryk spent nearly seven hours outside the station to retrieve, mount, and deploy the 63‑foot (19 m) carbon‑composite array on the starboard side of the truss. After a brief shunt‑current anomaly was resolved, the array unfurled without a motor, adding up to 30 percent more electrical power. Completing the iROSA program marks the end of a 15‑year effort to modernize the station’s power system.

<Callout type="takeaway">The final iROSA adds up to 30% more electricity to the ISS, completing a decade‑long solar upgrade.</Callout>

## What happened
The EVA began at 8:36 a.m. EST on 5 Nov, with Menon and Kutryk retrieving the 750‑lb (340 kg) iROSA from a temporary stowage platform using Canadarm2 and securing it to the pre‑installed bracket kit on the 3B power channel of the starboard truss. They then routed the power and data cables to integrate the array with the station’s electrical system.

During the connection phase, Mission Control reported that the shunt current measured at the array was only about half of the expected value, prompting the crew to pause deployment and wait for the station to enter Earth’s shadow so they could safely inspect the connectors.

Ground engineers verified the shunt current after the inspection, giving a “go for deploy” clearance. The astronauts released two bolts, and the iROSA unfolded to its full 63‑foot length in roughly ten minutes, using only the stored energy in its rolled‑up carbon composite booms.

## Why it matters
The new iROSA boosts the ISS’s total power generation by up to 30 %, supporting higher‑energy experiments, more robust life‑support systems, and the upcoming “Max Power” ground demonstration. By replacing aging legacy panels, the upgrade extends the station’s operational lifespan and provides critical redundancy for future commercial and research activities in low‑Earth orbit.

## How to think about it
When planning future power upgrades, engineers should factor the increased electricity budget into experiment scheduling and life‑support margins, while also accounting for the added mass and integration time of EVAs. Continuous monitoring of shunt‑current levels before release is essential to avoid electrical hazards, and contingency plans should include shadow‑period inspections if anomalies arise.
