The annual NASA Space Apps Challenge just shattered its own participation records, pulling in 181,835 registrants who gathered at 593 events worldwide on Nov. 14‑15. This surge represents a 59 % jump over the previous year and marks the fifth consecutive year of growth. The event invites teams to tackle 14 NASA‑driven challenges using open datasets, ranging from Earth observation to deep‑space exploration. By mobilizing a worldwide community of technologists, students, and enthusiasts, the hackathon demonstrates how open science can spark creative solutions to real‑world problems. The scale of the effort underscores the growing appetite for hands‑on involvement in space research.

<Callout type="takeaway">The record‑breaking Space Apps Challenge proves that open NASA data can unite millions to address Earth and space challenges.</Callout>

## What happened
On Nov. 14‑15 NASA hosted its sixth‑annual Space Apps Challenge, registering 181,835 participants across 593 local events in more than 190 countries. Since its inception in 2012 the program has engaged over 600,000 people, and this year’s turnout was 59 % higher than the prior edition. Teams worked on one of 14 themed challenges, submitting prototype solutions before the deadline, with ten global winners to be announced in January.

The challenge leveraged datasets from NASA and 17 international space‑agency partners, all signatories of the Artemis Accord, including ESA, JAXA, ISRO, and the Canadian Space Agency. Three new partner agencies—Angola’s GGPEN, Brazil’s AEB, and Argentina’s CONAE—also contributed original challenges, expanding the geographic and scientific scope of the event.

## Why it matters
The surge in participation signals a broadening public appetite for hands‑on space science and demonstrates the effectiveness of open‑data initiatives in driving grassroots innovation. By exposing diverse skill sets to real NASA problems, the hackathon cultivates a pipeline of talent that can feed future missions and Earth‑science projects. International collaboration through shared challenges also strengthens diplomatic ties and aligns global research priorities under the Artemis Accord.

## How to think about it
If you’re interested in contributing, start by exploring NASA’s open‑data portals and identifying a challenge that matches your skills. Join a local event or form a virtual team to collaborate on a prototype, keeping the scope realistic for a two‑day sprint. Treat the experience as a learning opportunity—document your process, seek feedback from mentors, and consider how your solution could be refined for future development or submission to related NASA programs.

<Callout type="warning">Not every prototype will be funded or turned into an operational mission; participants should view the hackathon as a learning platform rather than a guaranteed path to implementation.</Callout>
