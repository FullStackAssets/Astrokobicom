NASA has opened the BPS Digital AVATAR Challenge, inviting researchers to create digital twin models of astronaut bone marrow responses. The challenge builds on the AVATAR experiment that flew on Artemis II, where organ‑on‑a‑chip devices made from crew cells were returned after a lunar flyby. Participants must develop computational frameworks that integrate multi‑omics, physiological, and environmental data to predict how each astronaut’s immune system will react in deep space. Success could enable personalized medical kits for long‑duration missions, a critical step as NASA eyes crewed journeys to Mars.

<Callout type="takeaway">Personalized digital twins could become the cornerstone of astronaut health protection for deep‑space travel.</Callout>

## What happened
The AVATAR (A Virtual Astronaut Tissue Analog Response) investigation flew aboard Artemis II and returned organ‑on‑a‑chip bone marrow devices constructed from the cells of the crew after a 10‑day lunar flyby on April 10, 2026. This marked the first time an astronaut’s own tissue was used in a microgravity‑based organ‑on‑a‑chip model, providing real‑time biological data on how spaceflight stressors affect hematopoietic function.

NASA’s Biological and Physical Sciences Division has now launched the BPS Digital AVATAR Challenge, asking the scientific community to build a computational digital twin of that bone‑marrow chip. The required model must ingest diverse multi‑modal datasets—genomics, proteomics, metabolomics, physiological metrics, and environmental parameters—and output individualized predictions of immune and hematopoietic responses for each astronaut.

Proposals are expected to leverage established computational biology methods alongside modern digital‑twin architectures, enabling iterative refinement as new empirical data become available. The challenge emphasizes comparative analysis that separates crew‑specific effects from common stressor‑induced changes.

## Why it matters
Personalized health strategies are essential for the safety of crews on deep‑space missions where medical evacuation is impossible. By predicting how each astronaut’s bone marrow will respond, mission planners can tailor countermeasures, drug regimens, and medical kits to individual needs, reducing the risk of immune suppression or blood‑cell disorders.

Beyond crew health, the challenge accelerates the broader field of computational physiology, offering tools that could translate to terrestrial medicine, such as personalized cancer therapies or immune‑modulating treatments. It also demonstrates how high‑fidelity digital twins can integrate complex, multi‑scale data—a capability that will be vital for future habitats on the Moon or Mars.

## How to think about it
Approach the challenge as a stepwise workflow: first, curate a comprehensive dataset that combines the AVATAR chip’s multi‑omics readouts with astronaut physiological logs and mission environmental parameters. Next, select a digital‑twin platform—such as OpenSim, Physiome, or a custom agent‑based framework—and map each data stream to model inputs. Validate early versions against ground‑based analog experiments, then iteratively refine the model as in‑flight data become available. Collaborate with NASA’s BPS team to ensure alignment with mission constraints and to access any publicly released datasets.

<Callout type="warning">Do not rely on a digital twin’s predictions for critical medical decisions until the model has been rigorously validated against independent biological data.</Callout>
